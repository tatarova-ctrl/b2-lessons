🎯Presentation Skills

How to give a presentation people actually remember. Highlighted items are phrasal verbs and collocations — learn them and use them.

The Speaker Everyone Remembers

★ Naomi Ferreira · Presentation coach · Trains business leaders across Europe

I've watched thousands of presentations. Most of them fall flat. Smart, hard-working professionals stand up, read off their slides, and lose the room within a minute. They didn't fail because they weren't intelligent. They failed because nobody ever taught them that a presentation isn't about your slides. It's about whether you can quietly hold the attention of strangers for fifteen minutes.

Let's start with the beginning. How you kick off a talk decides everything. The first thirty seconds are when the audience silently decides whether to listen or to mentally check out. Don't open with “Hello, I'm Naomi, today I'd like to talk about…”. Open with a question, a number, or a tiny story. Warm up the room like a host, not a teacher.

“Your audience will forget your slides within an hour. They will remember how you made them feel for years.”

Then comes the structure. Great speakers walk the audience through a clear path. They build up to a main point, support it with one or two examples, and circle back to it at the end. Bad speakers jump around, throw in too much information, and confuse the room. Keep it simple. If you can't explain your idea in one sentence, you don't really know it yet.

The hardest skill is to get your point across without filling every second with words. Pauses are your friend. Many nervous speakers rush through their slides because silence feels uncomfortable. But silence gives the audience time to think, and it makes you sound calm and senior. Slow down. Breathe. Look at three different faces in the room.

Then there's the relationship with your slides. Don't read off them. The audience can read faster than you can speak. Use slides as wallpaper, not as a script. The best presenters glance at a slide, then look at the people. Their slides are simple: one big idea each, sometimes a photo, almost no text. If you need to fit ten lines on a slide, you have ten slides, not one.

When the inevitable problem arrives — a tough question, a technical issue, an audience member who talks over you — stay calm. Smile. Take a breath. “That's a great question. Let me come back to it after the next slide” is a complete and respectful sentence. Don't try to blag your way through an answer you don't know.

And finally, the ending. Most speakers run out of time at the end and quietly mumble, “So, yeah, thank you.” Don't be most speakers. Wrap up with a clear, simple takeaway. “If you remember one thing today, remember this.” Then stop. Take questions. Stand your ground when challenged, but never argue. Try this in your next talk: spend more time on your opening 60 seconds and closing 60 seconds than on everything in between. People remember beginnings and endings. The middle is just the bridge.

🎙️The Art of Speaking & Voice

The voice you use matters more than the words you choose. Highlighted items are real phrasal verbs and collocations.

The Voice You Use, Not the Words You Choose

★ Luca Bianchi · Voice coach · Trains TV presenters and senior executives

Most people focus on what they say. The professionals I coach quickly learn a harder truth: how you say it matters far more. The same sentence can land beautifully or fall flat depending on your voice. A nervous speaker can ruin a brilliant idea in three seconds. A calm one can get away with a thin idea for ten minutes. The good news is that voice is a skill, not a gift. You can build it.

Let's start with pace. Most nervous speakers speak too fast. Their brain is running ahead, their mouth is trying to catch up, and the audience can't process anything. The fix is uncomfortable: slow down. Pause after important sentences. Take a breath before answering a question. A small silence sounds calm and confident, even when you're terrified inside.

“Your voice tells the audience how to feel about your words. Even a brilliant sentence sounds boring if you mumble it.”

Then there's volume. People often trail off at the end of sentences. They start strongly and quietly fade out. The audience hears half the message. Train yourself to finish every sentence with the same energy you started it. If somebody at the back of the room can't hear you, speak up — even one notch louder.

Many speakers mumble because they're afraid of being heard. Open your mouth more than feels normal. Move your jaw. Pronounce the consonants clearly. People often think they have a quiet voice when they really have a closed mouth. Project your voice toward the back wall, not toward the floor.

Tone is the next layer. Speak in one flat tone and you'll send the audience to sleep, however clever your content. Use small changes in pitch to show what's important. Slow down on the key phrase. Drop your voice slightly on the punchline. Listen to any podcast host you love and notice their tiny voice changes — that's not natural talent; that's craft.

Then there's the moment everyone dreads. You dry up. You forget what you were going to say. Your hands sweat. You stumble over your words. The trick: don't apologise. Don't get tongue-tied trying to recover quickly. Just pause, breathe, smile, and say: “Let me try that again.” The audience won't punish you. They actually warm to a speaker who handles small problems calmly.

And finally, what you come across as. Confidence isn't loud. It's slow, calm, and clear. The most powerful speakers I know are not the most extroverted. They are the ones who learned to use their voice like an instrument: deliberate pace, gentle pauses, and a quiet refusal to fill every second with sound. Practise reading three pages out loud every day for two weeks. You won't believe the difference. Your voice is half your message. Find your voice, and people will start to listen to you in rooms where you used to be invisible.

👂Listening & Understanding

The most underrated communication skill of all — really listening. Phrasal verbs and collocations are in gold.

The Skill Nobody Practises

★ Yara Hassan · Communication trainer · 18 years inside large organisations

We spend years learning to read, write and speak. Almost nobody is taught how to listen. And yet, after eighteen years inside large organisations, I can tell you the colleagues who get on best with everyone are not the loudest or the smartest. They are the ones who really listen. They notice what most people miss, and they remember what most people forget.

Modern life is engineered to make listening difficult. Phones buzz. Notifications pop up. Our minds jump ahead to what we want to say. Studies suggest the average adult zones out roughly every twelve seconds in a conversation. We're not deaf. We're just not really there.

“Most people don't listen with the intent to understand. They listen with the intent to reply.”

Real listening starts with the body. Put your phone face down. Turn slightly toward the speaker. Stop scanning the room. People feel the difference within seconds. Even small acts — nodding along, gentle eye contact, an occasional “hmm” — tell the speaker you're with them. Without these tiny signals, even a perfect listener seems uninterested.

Then there's the mental side. Stop jumping in to finish other people's sentences. Stop preparing your reply while they're still talking. Try to take in what they're actually saying, including the things they're not saying directly. Most people don't tell you the real problem in the first sentence. They build up to it slowly. If you cut in too fast, you'll never hear it.

Good listeners pick up on small clues. A hesitation. A change of tone. The exact word someone chose, when another word was available. “I'm ‘fine’” is rarely the same as “I'm great.” The skill of reading between the lines is mostly the skill of slowing down enough to hear what's quietly there.

When you don't understand, say so. “Sorry, I'm not following what you're saying — can you say that again?” is a sign of confidence, not weakness. Many people nod politely when they're lost, and then make a small decision based on something they never properly understood. Ask follow-up questions. “Can you give me an example?” “What happened next?” “What did that feel like?” Questions like these turn a one-way speech into a real conversation.

There's also the dark side of listening: when to tune out politely. Some people will talk forever if you let them. The trick is to listen actively for a few minutes, then gently steer the conversation: “I'd love to hear more, but I have to get back to something at 4. Can we pick this up later?” That's not rude. That's adult.

Finally, remember the surprising power of being heard. Most people walk through their day feeling slightly unheard at home, at work, in shops. If you become the person who really listens — even for two minutes — you become genuinely rare. People will trust you, recommend you, and remember you. Listening is the cheapest, kindest superpower in modern professional life. Switch off the noise. Listen out for the real message. The rest will follow.

🎧Podcasts & Learning by Ear

How podcasts can become your second university. Phrasal verbs and collocations are in gold.

Free University in Your Headphones

★ Marco Vidal · Podcast producer & lifelong learner · Listens to 8 shows a week

Twenty years ago, if you wanted to learn from the best minds in the world, you went to expensive universities or rare bookshops. Today, you put on headphones during your walk to work and tune in for free. Podcasts have quietly become one of the most powerful learning tools in modern adult life. The hard part is no longer access. The hard part is choosing well.

Let's start with how to listen. Don't just put on background noise. Pick one show you actually find interesting, and dive into it properly. Most podcasts get really good around episode 20, when the hosts have found their voice. So if a show seems flat at the start, don't give up immediately. Catch up on a few key episodes and you'll quickly know whether it's for you.

“A good podcast doesn't tell you what to think. It quietly teaches you how a smart person thinks while they think.”

There are roughly four kinds of podcast worth your time. Interview shows like The Tim Ferriss Show or How I Built This are slow, long conversations with people who built something. Deep-dive shows like Hidden Brain or Freakonomics Radio dig into one topic per episode using research and storytelling. News explainers like The Daily or The Rest is Politics help you keep up with the world without doomscrolling. And narrative podcasts like Serial or This American Life get you hooked on a single story across many episodes.

For learning, slow podcasts beat fast ones. A 90-minute interview where someone explains how they actually do their work will teach you more than ten short clips. The big takeaways come from people thinking out loud, getting stuck, changing their mind. That's what an interview show offers that a TikTok video never can.

But podcasts aren't magic. You can listen for a hundred hours and not improve at anything. To really pick up tips, take notes on a few key ideas. Pause when something hits you. Try one small thing the next morning. The professionals who learn most from podcasts treat them like classes, not like wallpaper.

There's also the language side. For English learners, podcasts are gold. You hear real, natural English — phrasal verbs, hesitation, accents, casual collocations — that no textbook can capture. Start with podcasts that have transcripts, so you can look up what you didn't catch. Listen back to the same episode the next day. Most people are shocked by how much more they catch the second time.

Be careful, though. Podcasts can become a way of feeling busy and intelligent while doing nothing. If your queue is growing faster than your understanding, cut back. Unsubscribe from anything you've stopped enjoying. Branch out into one or two topics outside your comfort zone every few months — you'll be surprised how much you change. Headphones are the cheapest education in history. Use them wisely, and within two years, you can become an informed person on five subjects you knew nothing about. Your future self will quietly thank you.
