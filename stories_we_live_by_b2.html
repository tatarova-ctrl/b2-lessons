🎬Watching Movies & Genres

How we really watch films now — and what a good one still does to us. Highlighted items are phrasal verbs and collocations.

The Screen That Chooses You Back

★ Marco Vidal · Film critic & former festival programmer · Watches 250 films a year

When I was a teenager, watching a film was an event. You looked up the times in the newspaper, you queued up outside the cinema, you sat in the dark with strangers, and the story took over your life for two hours. Today, most of us watch films curled up on a sofa, phone in hand, sometimes flicking through three streaming apps at the same time. Films have never been easier to access. They have also never been easier to ignore.

Let's start with genres. Every film sits in one or two categories that shape what we expect. Rom-coms promise a happy ending. Thrillers promise tension. Horror promises fear you can safely enjoy. Sci-fi promises new worlds. Documentaries promise something true. Biopics promise a real life told well. Animated films promise something children can love without their parents getting bored. Good filmmakers know the rules of their genre and quietly bend them. Great filmmakers break them cleanly.

“You don't remember the plot of the film that changed you. You remember how you felt walking out of the cinema. The rest is craft.”

Then there's the language of film. A plot is what happens. A character arc is how a person changes. Pacing is the rhythm of the story. Cinematography is how the film looks. The score is the music that tells you how to feel. When people say a film is “well made,” they usually mean these elements come together without drawing attention to themselves. When a film is bad, one of them is always letting the side down.

The biggest revolution of the last decade is streaming. Netflix, Amazon, Disney+, Apple TV and others put thousands of films at your fingertips. This is wonderful. It's also strange. Cinemas are quieter. Small films struggle to break through the noise. Algorithms quietly decide what shows up on your homepage, and, over time, they narrow what you watch. Two people using the same app may live in completely different film worlds.

Then there's the modern habit of binge-watching. You settle in for one episode, and four hours later you're still there, tired, slightly ashamed, but wanting one more. Series are now written to be binged: each episode ends on a cliffhanger designed to reel you in. Cinema tries to fight back with three-hour films that demand your full attention. Both are competing for the same thing: your evening.

There's also the cinema experience. Big screens, quiet crowds, popcorn, no phone. Something happens in a dark room with a hundred strangers that never happens at home. That's why comedies feel funnier at the cinema, and horror films are actually scary. If you love films, get out to the cinema once a month. Streaming has quietly convinced us that this old ritual doesn't matter. It does.

The most useful skill for a modern viewer is curation. There are too many films to see them all. Follow a critic whose taste you trust. Keep a personal “to watch” list. Rule out anything you're only half-interested in. Be willing to give up on a bad film after twenty minutes; your life is short and someone else made something better.

If you take one idea, take this. A film is a small collaboration between the story on the screen and the person watching. If you're tired, distracted, checking your phone, and half-asleep, no film in the world can reach you. Watch fewer films. Watch them properly. Switch off the phone, dim the lights, and let the story do what it was made to do. That's how films quietly change your life.

📚Reading Books & Genres

How reading real books quietly reshapes the mind — and how to build the habit in a distracted world. Targets are in gold.

The Habit That Rearranges Your Head

★ Priya Nair · Author & former literature professor · Publishes a novel every four years

In an age of endless short videos, reading a book feels almost radical. You sit still. You focus on one thing for thirty minutes at a time. You let someone else's thoughts quietly move into your head. Reading isn't magical. It's just powerful. After twenty years of writing and teaching, I can tell you the same thing every serious reader eventually notices: the people who read regularly think differently. They notice more. They express themselves more carefully. Nothing on your phone will do this for you.

Let's start with fiction. Novels don't teach facts; they teach empathy. When you spend three hundred pages inside another person's head, you quietly learn to understand people in real life. That's why fiction readers, in study after study, are shown to be better at reading emotions and situations. Fiction sits at the heart of every honest education, and yet it's the first thing most adults drop when they get busy.

“The habit isn't reading. The habit is being a person willing to sit alone with a book, and to be changed by it.”

Fiction comes in many genres. Literary fiction tries to say something serious about being human. Historical fiction puts you inside another century. Crime and thrillers keep you turning pages until 1 a.m. Fantasy and sci-fi build entire worlds from scratch. Romance is bigger than any other genre and quietly outsells them all. Snobs like to look down on romance and fantasy; the truth is that a great genre novel does exactly what a great literary novel does: it takes you somewhere and leaves you slightly changed.

Then there's non-fiction. Biographies and memoirs let you live another life. Popular science makes the world genuinely more interesting. History shows how we got here. Essays help you think. Self-help is a huge and mixed category: at its best, it changes lives; at its worst, it repeats the same thin advice with a shiny cover. Choose carefully.

The biggest question modern readers ask is paper or digital. The honest answer is: whichever you'll actually read. E-readers are wonderful for travel and for people with bad eyesight. Paper books are quietly better for concentration and memory. Audiobooks let you “read” while commuting or cooking. There is no correct format; there's only the one you'll actually pick up.

Let's talk about the reading habit. Most adults who say they don't have time to read have plenty of time. They just spend it scrolling. Even 20 minutes a day, kept up, is 20 to 30 books a year. The people who read a lot don't have secret hours; they've quietly decided that reading is their default when they're waiting, resting, or falling asleep. Keep a book with you. Dip into it whenever you have five minutes. It quietly adds up.

There's also the difficult truth about giving up on books. Life is short. If you're 50 pages in and hating it, put it down. Reading isn't school. You don't get a prize for finishing something you don't enjoy. The best readers I know are ruthless about abandoning books that aren't for them, and slow to recommend a book they haven't settled into properly themselves.

Finally, remember that books shape you slowly. You may not remember the exact plot of a novel you read at 22. But something in it quietly reorganised how you saw the world. Books are one of the few technologies that let another human being — sometimes long dead — sit quietly beside you and reshape your thinking, one paragraph at a time.

If you take one idea, take this. Pick up a real book tonight. Read for 20 minutes before bed. Do it again tomorrow. That's the whole method. Nothing dramatic. Just the quiet daily habit of letting other minds into yours. In a year, you'll be a different reader, and a slightly different person.

🌟Books That Actually Change You

Real book recommendations across categories — the ones you'll thank yourself for reading. Targets are in gold.

Books That Change You Twice

★ Emma Rowntree · Literary editor · Spent 20 years recommending the right book to the right person

After 20 years of recommending books, I've learned that people don't want lists. They want the right book at the right time. Still, some books quietly change almost everyone who reads them properly. Not because they're fashionable. Because they patiently do something to your thinking. Here are a few I keep coming back to, across different categories. Read one properly this year, and it will pay you back for the next decade.

Let's start with modern classics. If you haven't picked up Never Let Me Go by Kazuo Ishiguro, you're missing one of the most quietly devastating novels of the last 30 years. The Remains of the Day, by the same author, is another slow, perfect book. Beloved by Toni Morrison is one of the great American novels of the twentieth century. A Little Life by Hanya Yanagihara is huge, difficult, and worth every page for those who can take it on. These aren't easy reads. They are the ones you'll keep coming back to.

“Some books hit you at the first reading. Others quietly change you the second time, when you finally understand what you were reading.”

In non-fiction and ideas, three books have been genuinely influential in the last decade. Sapiens by Yuval Noah Harari opened many readers' eyes to the sweep of human history. Thinking, Fast and Slow by Daniel Kahneman is dense, but it will change the way you think about your own decisions. Man's Search for Meaning by Viktor Frankl is short, tough, and still one of the wisest books ever written on how to live. These three alone will teach you more than most university courses.

For work and productivity, avoid the huge self-help section. Read fewer, better books. Deep Work by Cal Newport is genuinely useful for anyone with a job that requires focus. Atomic Habits by James Clear is the best modern book on habits. The Almanack of Naval Ravikant is a small, strange collection of ideas about wealth, time and happiness that you'll probably dip into once a year. These will save you from a hundred worse books in the same category.

In fiction that reads like a thriller, try The Remains of the Day (again), Klara and the Sun, Circe by Madeline Miller, The Overstory by Richard Powers, or anything by Kazuo Ishiguro. These are page-turners that also stay with you for years. Genre fiction can hold its own as literature. Don't let anyone talk you out of reading it.

For essays and thinking, Bird by Bird by Anne Lamott (technically about writing, actually about life) is a gentle miracle. Meditations by Marcus Aurelius, written 1,900 years ago by a Roman emperor to himself, is still one of the best books on staying calm and useful in a chaotic world. Dip into it whenever life feels hard.

For science and understanding, The Body: A Guide for Occupants by Bill Bryson is warm, funny and full of amazing facts. The Emperor of All Maladies by Siddhartha Mukherjee is a beautifully written history of cancer that will open your eyes to how modern medicine actually works. Why We Sleep by Matthew Walker will probably change the way you plan your evenings.

For books on work and creativity, my quiet favourite is Show Your Work by Austin Kleon: a short, punchy little book that will change how you share what you do. If you want a longer read, Range by David Epstein makes the case for being a generalist in a specialist world.

There are books I've read four times and still find something new in. There are also books I couldn't put down at the time but haven't thought about since. The books that shape you are usually the ones you were slightly not ready for when you first read them.

If you take one idea, take this. Don't burn through 20 forgettable books this year. Pick out three real ones from the list above. Read them slowly. Read one twice. In five years, you'll thank yourself. The books on this list are not a passing fashion. They are quietly waiting for you.

🎭Music, Film & Book Festivals

Why we still travel to fields, cinemas and bookshops to feel something together. Targets are in gold.

Why Half a Million People Stand in a Field

★ Isabel Ortega · Culture journalist · Covers 15 festivals a year

In a world where you can watch anything, hear anything and read anything from your sofa, hundreds of thousands of people still travel every summer to stand in a muddy field in Somerset, or queue outside a cinema in Cannes, or fly to Edinburgh in August for three weeks of theatre. Festivals should be dying. They're not. In fact, they're growing. Something about being physically in a place, surrounded by strangers who love the same thing, is genuinely irreplaceable.

Let's start with music festivals. Glastonbury in England is famous for its scale, its mud and its politics. Coachella in California is famous for the artists, the fashion, and the sunset. Tomorrowland in Belgium is famous for electronic music and huge stages. Roskilde in Denmark is famous for its atmosphere. Fuji Rock in Japan is famous for being polite and beautifully organised. Each one sells out in minutes. Each one has its own tribe.

“A festival is not really about the music. It's about the version of yourself you become for three days in a field.”

Getting to a festival is half the story. People line up for tickets months in advance. They camp out for days. They put together outfits, meet friends they only see once a year, and quietly psych themselves up for the biggest night of their year. When the first band steps on stage, and 100,000 people scream at once, something genuinely powerful happens. You cannot get that from a streaming app.

Film festivals are quieter but just as intense. Cannes in May is the most glamorous. Venice in September launches many Oscar contenders. Sundance in the Utah snow is the home of independent film. Berlin is politically serious. Toronto is where audiences vote with genuine enthusiasm. These festivals shape what the world will watch a year later. Small films break through here. Careers begin here. Fashion is discussed here. Deals are struck at breakfast.

For many of the smaller film festivals — local, national, genre-specific — the atmosphere is closer to a book club than a red carpet. You watch three films in a day, argue about them at dinner, and bump into the director in the coffee queue the next morning. If you love film, go to one small festival before you die. It will change how you watch every film after.

Then there are book festivals. Hay Festival in Wales gathers hundreds of writers under white tents in a tiny town for two weeks. Edinburgh International Book Festival runs at the same time as the world's largest arts festival, drawing 800,000 visitors in three weeks. Cheltenham, Jaipur, Frankfurt, the Sharjah International Book Fair: each has its own personality. You go to hear an author read one paragraph, and end up buying five books you didn't know existed.

Then there are combined arts festivals. Edinburgh Fringe is the biggest in the world: three thousand shows, thousands of comedians, actors and dancers. SXSW in Texas mixes music, tech, and film in one strange, energising week. Burning Man, in the Nevada desert, is closer to a giant art project than a festival, and quietly changes people who go with the right attitude.

But festivals have a dark side too. Tickets are expensive. Overtourism hits famous festival cities hard. Line-ups often sell out before you've even decided. Small festivals sometimes get bought by giant companies and quietly lose their soul. And you'll come home dirty, tired, and probably slightly poorer than you should be.

But if you go into a festival with the right expectations, something quietly beautiful happens. Strangers help you find your friends. Musicians step off stage and hang out at the bar. Writers give real answers to student questions. Films you'd never have watched at home catch you off guard and stay with you for years.

If you take one idea, take this. Once a year, get out of your normal life and go somewhere with thousands of other people who love a thing you love. Music, film, books, comedy — it doesn't matter which. Line up, camp out, catch a set, soak up the vibe, and come home slightly changed. The festival won't be perfect. That's not the point. The point is that for three days, you remembered you were part of something bigger than your algorithm.
