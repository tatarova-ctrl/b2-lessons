📖Reading text

A futurist takes us through the technologies that will completely transform how we get around in the coming decades. Target language is in amber bold.

"Get Ready — Your Daily Commute Won't Recognise Itself"

★ Mobility & Future · 5-minute read

Imagine waking up in 2040. You ask your phone to order a ride, and within three minutes, an electric, self-driving car with no steering wheel pulls up outside your home. While it takes you to work, you read the news, answer some emails, or watch an episode of something. Above your head, delivery drones are silently dropping off groceries to your neighbours. In the distance, you can see a hyperloop station where, last weekend, you took a 30-minute trip to a city 600 km away. Science fiction? Maybe. But every single one of these technologies is already in some stage of development, and the way we move is about to change beyond recognition.

Let's start with the obvious. Electric vehicles have already gone mainstream. Just five years ago, EVs were a niche product for tech enthusiasts. Today, more than one in five new cars sold in Europe is fully electric, and that number is climbing rapidly. Range anxiety — the fear that your battery will run out before you reach a charger — is gradually disappearing as charging infrastructure expands and battery technology improves. Most experts agree it's not a question of if EVs will replace petrol cars, but how quickly.

The next big leap is autonomous driving. We're not quite there yet — fully self-driving cars still struggle with bad weather, complex city traffic and unpredictable human behaviour. But progress has been faster than most people realise. In several US cities, robotaxi services already operate without a human driver. The biggest barrier today isn't really the technology — it's regulation, public trust, and the very tricky question of who is responsible when something goes wrong. Honestly, the legal questions are almost harder than the engineering ones.

"The first generation of cars replaced horses. The next generation of cars will replace drivers." — transport analyst, MIT

Then there's the hyperloop — a tube-based transport system designed to shoot passengers at speeds of over 1,000 km/h between cities. The concept, popularised by Elon Musk back in 2013, has had a bumpy ride. Several companies went bust after struggling to secure funding, and many engineers have argued that the costs are simply prohibitive. But the technology hasn't gone away. China, India and the UAE are all investing heavily in hyperloop research, and a working passenger line within a decade is no longer pure fantasy.

Drones are perhaps the most quietly revolutionary part of the story. Delivery drones are already in use in parts of the US and Australia, dropping off everything from coffee to medical supplies. Air taxis — small electric aircraft that can carry passengers — are scheduled to launch commercially in cities like Paris, Dubai and São Paulo in the next two or three years. Of course, the moment hundreds of these things are flying over a city, you have a whole new set of problems: noise, safety, and increasingly, airspace traffic management.

And then there's the green angle. Transport currently accounts for around a quarter of global CO₂ emissions. Every credible plan to tackle climate change involves decarbonising mobility — getting people out of petrol cars, off polluting aircraft, and onto cleaner alternatives. Public transport is being upgraded and electrified across Europe. Cities like Paris, Bogotá and Singapore are reclaiming streets from cars and giving them back to cyclists, walkers and trams. Car-free city centres are becoming the norm rather than the exception.

So is everything sunshine and rainbows? Not quite. New technologies always come with new problems. EVs require huge amounts of lithium and cobalt, often mined under questionable conditions. Self-driving systems raise privacy concerns because they constantly collect data. Drones could worsen inequality if only wealthy areas get fast deliveries. And honestly, all this innovation could mean very little if we don't tackle the real source of urban transport problems: poor planning, car dependency and underinvestment in public transit.

Still, optimism feels reasonable. Within a generation, the way we move around our cities — and across continents — is likely to look totally different from today. As one futurist put it: "For the first time in 100 years, transport innovation isn't just about going faster. It's about going smarter, cleaner, and together." Whether we get it right is up to us.

🚀 Phrases to debate technology & the future

Predicting: "I reckon that within 10 years…" / "It's only a matter of time before…" / "Don't be surprised if…"
Showing balance: "On the one hand… on the other…" / "It cuts both ways." / "There are real trade-offs."
Being sceptical: "I'm not 100% convinced…" / "It sounds great in theory, but…" / "I'll believe it when I see it."
Being optimistic: "I'm genuinely excited about…" / "This could be a real breakthrough." / "The potential is huge."
Closing: "All things considered…" / "At the end of the day…" / "The bottom line is…"

📝Grammar: Future Forms & Modals of Possibility

Two grammar areas you absolutely need to discuss future technology: future forms (will / going to / present continuous / future perfect) and modals of possibility (might, could, may, will, won't) for predictions.

🚄 Part 1 — Future forms

  • will → opinion / general prediction. I think EVs will replace petrol cars.
  • be going to → prediction based on present evidence; intentions. Look at this trend — drones are going to be everywhere.
  • present continuous → fixed plans. The city is launching a new tram next month.
  • future perfect (will have + past participle) → completed by a future point. By 2035, we will have phased out petrol cars.
  • future continuous (will be + -ing) → in progress at a future moment. This time next year, I'll be commuting in an EV.

🚄 Part 2 — Modals of possibility for predictions

  • will / won't = certain. EVs will dominate the market.
  • might / may / could = possible (50/50). Hyperloop might become real, but it's not guaranteed.
  • will probably / probably won't = likely. Air taxis will probably launch first in Dubai.
  • is bound to / is sure to = certain. Drone deliveries are bound to grow.
  • is unlikely to = probably not. Flying cars are unlikely to be common by 2030.
