🌐Software & Web Development

A senior developer on what writing code is actually like — once you stop being a junior. Target language is in gold.

Code Is Just the Beginning

★ Marina Volkova · Senior software engineer · Worked at three start-ups and one bank

I'll tell you a secret about software engineering that nobody mentions on bootcamp websites. Writing code is the easy part. Most of my day isn't typing. It's reading other people's code, fixing strange bugs, sitting in meetings, and explaining a small mistake that broke something at 2 a.m. The actual programming is maybe two hours out of eight. The rest is the slow, careful work that turns code into a real product.

When you're new, you imagine that a developer just writes features all day. In reality, every line of code is part of a longer journey. You write the change, you open a pull request, you wait for a code review from a senior colleague. They point out problems you didn't see. You rewrite parts. The tests run. Sometimes they fail. You debug, you push again. Only after all of this do you ship anything.

Good developers don't write more code. They write code that someone else can safely change in two years' time.

Bugs are part of life. A clever bug feels almost human — it appears only on Tuesdays, only for one user, only when they click in a certain order. The hardest skill in development isn't writing new code; it's reading old code well enough to fix it. Most of the world's most useful software is twenty years old and held together by people who patiently learn how it works.

Modern teams ship constantly. We use version control like Git to manage changes, CI/CD pipelines to test and release them, and monitoring tools to see if anything breaks once real users arrive. The phrase “it works on my machine” is now a tired joke for a reason. Real software has to work for thousands of people on different devices, with bad Wi-Fi, in countries you've never been to.

Then there's the human side. Stand-up meetings every morning. Discussions with product managers about priorities. Pair programming with a colleague when something is hard. Mentoring a junior, which often teaches you more than it teaches them. Engineering is a team sport. The people who become great are not the lonely geniuses of films. They are calm communicators who can disagree without drama.

And then there's the difficult bit nobody warns you about: maintenance. Old code, old bugs, technical debt from quick decisions made years ago. Beautiful new projects are rare. Most of the work is keeping existing systems alive and slightly improving them, week by week. The young developers I admire most are not the ones who say, “I built this in a weekend.” They are the ones who say, “I made this old, scary system 10% easier to work with.” That sentence is the real definition of seniority.

🤖AI & Machine Learning at Work

An ML engineer on what AI in a real team looks like — far less glamorous, far more useful. Target language is in gold.

The Colleague Who Never Sleeps

★ Daniel Park · Machine learning engineer · Five years building AI for retail and health

When people ask me what an AI engineer does, they expect stories from science-fiction films. The reality is much more boring — and much more interesting. Most of my time isn't training enormous models. It's cleaning data, fixing the same five errors, talking with product teams, and arguing about whether we should use AI for a problem at all. AI is the colleague who never sleeps. That doesn't mean it's always right.

The most common projects fall into two groups. Predictive models guess something: which customers might leave, which patients are at risk, when a machine will break. Generative AI produces something new: text, images, code, audio. Both depend on the same secret: data. With clean, useful data, ordinary algorithms win. With messy data, even the best model in the world looks stupid. Garbage in, garbage out is older than computers, and it's still the most important rule in the field.

AI doesn't replace thinking. It accelerates whatever thinking was already happening. If the thinking was lazy, AI just produces lazy answers faster.

Modern teams use AI in two very different ways. Some treat it like a calculator: small, focused tools inside their products — a recommendation engine, a search ranker, a translation service. Others treat it like a junior colleague: a large language model that can draft an email, summarise a document, suggest a function. Both are real engineering. Both need careful design, testing and monitoring.

The hardest part isn't the maths. It's deployment. Once a model is built, it has to run reliably at scale, handle weird inputs, and not say something embarrassing. Real teams put a lot of work into guardrails, evaluations and human review. The famous failures — chatbots saying offensive things, medical AIs giving dangerous advice — usually happen when teams shipped a clever model without the boring supporting structure.

Then there's the ethics. AI systems can quietly copy human bias hidden in the data. A hiring model trained on years of biased decisions will simply produce more biased decisions, much faster. The interesting AI engineers I know spend serious time thinking about who their model can hurt, who's underrepresented in the data, and what fairness would actually look like in their use case.

For everyone else: the practical advice is short. Learn one or two AI tools well. Don't treat AI output as truth; treat it as a fast first draft. Always keep a human in the loop for important decisions. And remember that the people who'll do best in the next decade are not the ones who use AI to think less. They are the ones who use AI to think faster, then check the work carefully — like the senior colleague we all want to be.

🔐Cybersecurity & Online Safety

A cybersecurity analyst on the door you didn't realise you left open. Target language is in gold.

The Door You Left Open

★ Amina Chen · Cybersecurity analyst · Defends 3,000 employees and their inboxes

I have a job most people don't think about until something goes wrong. I'm a cybersecurity analyst, which means I spend my days watching attacks that almost worked and trying to stop the ones still on their way. The first thing I'll tell you is the truth that most companies discover too late: the technology isn't usually the problem. People are. The weakest link in any system is the human who clicks the wrong link at 9 a.m. on a Monday.

The most common attack you'll meet is phishing. A fake email pretending to be your bank, your boss, or your delivery service, asking you to log in or open an attachment. They are getting better every month, especially with AI helping criminals write perfect English. The clue is almost always urgency. Real organisations rarely write, “Your account will be closed in 30 minutes.” Scammers do.

Most hacks are not James Bond. They are someone tired, distracted and in a hurry, clicking one link they shouldn't have.

The second classic is weak passwords. “Password123” is still embarrassingly common. Re-using the same password across sites is even worse: one breach and the attacker has the key to your whole life. A password manager and two-factor authentication (2FA) are the two cheapest, most powerful upgrades any user can make. Better safe than sorry.

Then there's ransomware, the nightmare of IT teams. A criminal group gets inside, encrypts all your files, and demands payment for the key. Hospitals, schools and city councils have all been hit. The cost is rarely the ransom itself; it's the weeks of downtime, lost data and damaged trust. Good backups, regularly tested, are the only real defence.

On the defensive side, modern teams use firewalls, antivirus, VPNs, intrusion detection, and constant security updates. But the most powerful tool is still training. The companies with the fewest incidents are the ones that run regular fake phishing tests and teach their staff to spot bad emails. A culture where people happily report a mistake is far safer than one where they hide it.

On a personal level, the rules aren't dramatic. Use long, unique passwords. Turn on 2FA everywhere. Update your devices when they ask. Be suspicious of any message that creates urgency. Cover your laptop camera if it makes you sleep better. None of this is glamorous. All of it works. Security is mostly a thousand small, boring habits done well — with the occasional wake-up call that reminds you why they matter.

☁️Cloud, Data & Remote Work

A cloud architect on the building that runs your life — the one you'll never visit. Target language is in gold.

Your Office Lives in a Building You'll Never Visit

★ Felipe Almeida · Cloud architect · Designs systems that serve millions

Every email you send, every photo you back up, every video call — all of it lives in buildings you'll probably never see. Huge, cold, humming warehouses called data centres, filled with thousands of servers, somewhere outside a city like Dublin, Singapore or Virginia. People say “the cloud” as if it's a mysterious sky. It isn't. The cloud is just somebody else's computer, in a building you don't own.

Cloud computing has quietly changed the tech industry more than almost anything else. Twenty years ago, every company had to buy and look after its own servers. Today, most run on platforms like AWS, Google Cloud or Azure. You can spin up a powerful new server in two minutes, pay only for what you use, and shut it down again when you're done. This is why a small team of five engineers can now serve millions of users.

You can rent more computing power for a coffee than the entire Apollo programme had. The question is no longer whether you can build it. It's what you'll build with it.

Data is the other big change. Every product now collects information — what users click, where they pause, what they search for. That data lives in databases, gets cleaned by data engineers, and gets used by data analysts to make decisions. A modern company without data is like a ship without instruments. Whether that's healthy is another question entirely.

Then there's DevOps — the modern way of running software. Instead of one team writing code and another team running it, the same team does both. They use automation, monitoring and CI/CD pipelines to deploy small changes quickly and safely. When something goes wrong — and it always does — alerts wake up someone on call. The famous saying is, “you build it, you run it.” No more throwing it over the wall.

The cloud also enabled the modern remote work movement. With everything in browsers and shared documents, remote teams can ship serious products from twenty time zones. Async communication — writing things down properly instead of needing constant meetings — is now a core skill. The best remote teams I work with feel more focused and less stressed than the average office team.

But there's a quiet warning here too. The cloud is convenient, but it concentrates enormous power in a handful of giant companies. When one of them has an outage, a third of the internet goes down for an afternoon. Costs can spiral if you don't watch them. And your data is in someone else's hands. So learn the basics, design for failure, keep good backups, and remember: the cloud is just somebody else's computer. The smart engineer treats it with respect, not magic.
