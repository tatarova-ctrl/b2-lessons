"Why Do We Punish? The Question Modern Justice Can't Quite Answer"

★ Society & Justice · feature article

For as long as humans have lived in groups, they have argued about what to do with people who break the rules. Should we lock them up and throw away the key? Should we try to rehabilitate them? Should we punish to deter others, or simply to make society feel that justice has been served? Two thousand years of philosophy — from Plato to Foucault — have failed to produce a clear answer. And yet every day, in every country, courts hand down sentences, juries reach verdicts, and prisons fill up. The question of why we punish has never fully gone away.

Most modern justice systems try to balance four ideas. The first is retribution — the simple idea that wrongdoing deserves punishment. The second is deterrence — punishing one person so others don't commit similar crimes. The third is incapacitation — keeping offenders off the streets so they can't reoffend. And the fourth is rehabilitation — helping someone change so they don't return to a life of crime after release. Where the system strikes the balance between these four — and how well — varies enormously from country to country.

Take the contrast between, for example, Norway and the United States. The US has the highest incarceration rate in the developed world, with over two million people behind bars at any given time. Many prisons are overcrowded, conditions can be harsh, and the focus, broadly speaking, is on punishment. Norway, by contrast, has built a system around the philosophy that "the punishment is the loss of freedom — nothing more." Its prisons look more like university dormitories than cells. Inmates have access to education, work, therapy. And the results? Norway's recidivism rate — the percentage of released prisoners who reoffend — is around 20%. The American figure is over three times that.

"You cannot punish a person into becoming a better citizen. You have to give them the tools and the trust to become one." — Norwegian prison governor

Of course, things are rarely that simple. Critics of the Scandinavian model argue that it works precisely because Norway is a small, wealthy, equal society — not because of its prisons. They have a point. Nations facing serious organised crime, gang violence or political corruption can't simply copy and paste a model from a different cultural and economic context. Anyone who suggests otherwise is, frankly, being naïve.

And yet, even the staunchest defenders of tough sentencing have started to ask uncomfortable questions. Does locking someone up for ten years for a non-violent offence actually reduce crime — or does it simply create a more hardened, more isolated, more unemployable individual when they finally come out? Is a system that disproportionately punishes the poor, the mentally ill and ethnic minorities really delivering justice at all? In recent years, even some traditionally conservative voices have started to admit that the "tough on crime" approach has, in many places, failed to deliver.

Then there's the technological revolution that nobody is quite ready for. Police forces around the world are now using predictive algorithms, facial-recognition cameras and DNA databases at a scale that would have been unthinkable twenty years ago. On paper, this means more efficient policing and fewer wrongful convictions. In practice, it raises huge concerns about privacy, algorithmic bias and the kind of society we're building. Several countries have already paused or restricted these tools after evidence emerged that they could misidentify people of colour at significantly higher rates.

What about the victims? It's striking how often their voices are missing from these debates. Restorative justice — a relatively new approach that brings offenders and victims together to discuss the harm caused and agree on how to repair it — has been quietly gaining ground. Studies suggest that, in many cases, victims actually leave these processes feeling more heard, more respected and more at peace than they did after a traditional trial. Yet across most of the world, restorative justice remains the exception rather than the rule.

Perhaps the deepest question of all is this: are we trying to punish people, or are we trying to build a safer society? Most of us would say "both" — but the two goals don't always point in the same direction. Long sentences may satisfy the public's desire for revenge but do little, statistically, to reduce reoffending. Investing in mental health, education, social housing and addiction treatment may do more to cut crime rates than any prison policy — but they're slow, unspectacular, and politically much harder to sell.

What's clear is that justice is not a finished product. It's a conversation, an ongoing negotiation between fairness, safety, mercy and accountability. As one judge once put it: "Justice that doesn't ask hard questions of itself is no justice at all."

⚖ Phrases for thoughtful debate

Stating an opinion: "I'd argue that…" / "From my perspective…" / "It seems to me that…"
Showing balance: "On the one hand… on the other…" / "There are valid arguments on both sides." / "It cuts both ways."
Citing authority: "The evidence suggests…" / "Studies have shown that…" / "Experts argue that…"

Concluding: "All things considered…" / "At the end of the day…" / "When you weigh it all up…"

📝Grammar: Passive Voice & Conditionals

Two grammar areas you genuinely need to talk about crime and justice: passive voice (used everywhere in legal language and journalism) and conditionals (for hypothetical justice scenarios).

⚖ Part 1 — Passive voice in legal language

  • Legal English uses passive constantly because the action matters more than who did it: He was arrested.
  • Form: subject + BE + past participle (+ by + agent)
  • Past: The suspect was charged yesterday.
  • Present perfect: The man has been convicted of fraud.
  • Future: The verdict will be announced on Friday.
  • Modal: Justice must be served.
Active: The judge sentenced him to 10 years. Passive: He was sentenced to 10 years (by the judge).

⚖ Part 2 — Conditionals

  • 1st — real future: If you commit a crime, you'll face the consequences.
  • 2nd — hypothetical present: If I were a judge, I would focus on rehabilitation.
  • 3rd — past hypothetical: If the police had arrived earlier, the crime might have been prevented.
  • Mixed — past condition → present result: If she had been convicted, she would still be in prison today.
