📣Digital Marketing Fundamentals

A marketing director on how customers really find a brand online — and why most ads quietly fail. Target language is in gold.

Nobody Buys From a Stranger

★ Sara Marinković · Marketing director · Built three brands from zero to global

I'll tell you the sentence I write at the top of every new marketing plan: nobody buys from a stranger. The customer of 2026 doesn't see one of your ads and run to your shop. They see your name three or four times, in different places, over several weeks. They look at your website. They read one comment. They ignore you twice more. Then, one Tuesday morning, they finally convert. Modern marketing isn't about shouting louder. It's about being calmly present, again and again, until trust grows.

Every digital marketing plan I build sits on three legs. The first is SEO — search engine optimisation. When a customer types “best physio in Madrid”, you want to appear on the first page of Google. Good SEO is about clear websites, useful content and patience. It's slow, but the customers it brings are often the best ones, because they were actively searching for you.

The cheapest marketing in the world is to deserve the customer first. The most expensive is to chase them when you don't.

The second leg is paid ads. PPC on Google, plus social ads on Instagram, TikTok, LinkedIn, YouTube. Used well, they bring fast traffic. Used badly, they're a black hole for money. Successful companies don't throw money at it. They test small, measure the click-through rate and conversion rate, and slowly scale up the ads that actually work.

The third leg is organic social media and content marketing. This is your free space — posts, videos, articles, podcasts — where you build a personality, not just sell things. The brands I admire most don't behave like brands online. They sound like a smart, generous friend. They answer real questions. They show real people. They make customers feel seen, before they sell anything.

Behind all this sits the marketing funnel: the journey from a stranger who has never heard of you, to a curious visitor, to a happy customer. Each stage needs different content. Awareness at the top (“do I know this brand?”), consideration in the middle (“is it for me?”), and conversion at the bottom (“I'll buy”). Most brands shout “BUY NOW” at people who don't even know they exist yet. No wonder it doesn't work.

Finally, measure everything. Analytics are not optional. How many people land on your homepage? Where do they come from? Where do they leave? The team that wins is rarely the most creative one. It's the one that quietly looks at the numbers every week, kills what doesn't work, and doubles down on what does. Marketing is not a fireworks show. It's a long, patient conversation — with data on one side and humans on the other.

🎬Content & Video Creation

A content creator on what makes a video stop the scroll — and what doesn't. Target language is in gold.

The Three Seconds That Decide Everything

★ Carlos Mendes · Content creator · Built a 2-million-follower channel from his bedroom

Five years ago, I made my first video in my bedroom with a phone and a desk lamp. Last month, three million people watched a short of mine without me spending a single euro on ads. People always ask me what changed. The answer is the same boring secret nobody wants to hear: I learned to take the first three seconds of every video deadly seriously.

On every modern platform — TikTok, Instagram Reels, YouTube Shorts — you have about three seconds before the viewer's thumb scrolls away. Three seconds. If your hook is weak, the rest of your video, however brilliant, will never be seen. New creators spend an hour on the editing and one minute on the opening. The professionals do exactly the opposite.

Don't make a perfect video. Make a video the first three seconds of which a tired person at midnight cannot scroll past.

A good hook does three things. It promises something interesting (“The one mistake every beginner makes…”). It creates a tiny question in the viewer's head (“Wait, why is she doing that?”). And it shows clearly what kind of content you make. The hook is a contract: if you stay, this is what you'll get.

But hooks alone don't build a channel. Content strategy does. Pick a clear topic, a clear voice, a clear target audience. Don't make videos for “everyone”. The most successful creators make videos for one specific person and let everyone else watch by accident. “Marketing tips” goes nowhere. “Marketing tips for tiny dental clinics” quietly builds an empire.

Production matters less than people think. Good lighting and clean audio beat an expensive camera every single time. Viewers will forgive a shaky frame. They will not forgive bad sound. Spend twenty euros on a clip-on microphone before you spend two thousand on a fancy lens.

Storytelling is the secret of long-form content. Even a 30-second video can have a tiny structure: situation, problem, twist, lesson. Set the scene, show the trouble, surprise them, give them something useful. The brain is wired for stories. It tolerates information when wrapped in one and ignores it when not.

Then there's the unglamorous part: consistency. Most creators quit at month four. The people who reach a hundred thousand followers are not more talented; they post regularly when nobody is watching yet. Add a clear call to action (“follow for part two”), study your analytics, and adjust calmly. Your first hundred videos are practice. Your second hundred are when you really start. Most people quit at video forty. That's why the field is wide open for whoever stays.

🤖AI Tools in Marketing

A marketing tech lead on how AI is quietly rewriting the job — and where it still gets things spectacularly wrong. Target language is in gold.

Your New Junior Marketer Works 24/7

★ Priya Sharma · Marketing tech lead · Inside fast-growing agencies

Last year my team produced more content than ever before, with fewer people. The reason isn't magic. We hired AI as a quiet junior member of the team. AI bots now help us draft posts, summarise customer reviews, generate first ideas for ads, write the boring 80% of an email so the human can focus on the clever 20%. AI doesn't replace marketers. It replaces the lazy version of every marketer.

The most useful tools right now fall into three groups. Generative AI — ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini — for text, brainstorming, summaries and first drafts. Image and video tools — Midjourney, Runway, Sora — for visuals, mood boards and short clips. And automation tools — Zapier, Make, HubSpot — that connect everything: when a lead fills a form, the system sends an email, adds them to a list, tags them, alerts the salesperson, and books a call. None of these are new ideas. They are just suddenly cheap.

AI doesn't replace marketers. It replaces the lazy version of every marketer.

But the secret weapon is the prompt. A short, vague prompt gives short, vague results. A clear, detailed prompt — with the audience, tone, length, and an example — gives something genuinely useful. We've started writing prompts the way we used to write briefs for an agency. The better the brief, the better the output. Prompt engineering is now a real skill, not a buzzword.

AI is brilliant at certain tasks. It can summarise 200 reviews into five themes in two minutes. It can translate a campaign into eight languages by lunchtime. It can suggest twenty headline options when your brain has gone blank. For repetitive tasks at scale — tagging, sorting, first drafts — it is genuinely a game-changer.

But it also fails confidently. AI will happily hallucinate a statistic, invent a quote, or claim a competitor said something they didn't. It can also quietly produce “all brands sound the same” content, full of correct words but with no soul. The agencies winning right now are not the ones using AI most. They are the ones using it best: AI for speed, humans for taste.

There are ethical problems too. Should you tell customers when an email was written by AI? When is an AI “influencer” acceptable? Should AI-generated art credit the artists it was trained on? Smart marketing teams discuss these questions openly, instead of pretending they don't exist.

My honest advice is simple. Learn two or three AI tools well, not twenty badly. Treat every AI output as a first draft, not a final piece. Always keep a human in the loop for anything customer-facing. And remember: AI will only ever make you more of what you already are. A careful marketer becomes faster. A lazy marketer becomes louder. Choose carefully which one you want to be.

💬Customer Relationships & Community

A community manager on the moment marketing becomes something better — a real relationship. Target language is in gold.

Selling Once vs. Keeping Forever

★ Liam Murphy · Community & CX manager · Built loyal audiences for three direct-to-consumer brands

Most marketing books talk about how to sell something once. Real businesses live or die on whether the customer comes back. After ten years inside online brands, I can tell you the boring truth: keeping a customer is five times cheaper than finding a new one, and far more enjoyable. Yet most companies obsess over new leads and quietly ignore the customers they already have. It's like throwing a brilliant party and never inviting your real friends.

The first idea to take seriously is the customer journey. From the moment a stranger discovers your brand to the moment they're a five-year loyal fan, there are dozens of small touchpoints: the homepage, the first email, the unboxing, the chat with support, the second purchase, the moment they recommend you to a friend. Each touchpoint either builds trust or quietly destroys it. Brands lose customers far more often through small frictions than through big disasters.

Loyalty is built in the small moments where you didn't have to try, and you did anyway.

Then there's customer service, which I'd rename “marketing in disguise”. A patient, kind, fast support team is one of the most powerful growth tools a brand has. People talk for years about the customer service that surprised them. A two-line email reply with real warmth is worth more than a million-euro ad. Word of mouth from happy customers is still the cheapest and most trusted marketing in the world.

Email marketing is the quiet hero of customer relationships. Social media platforms own your followers; email is the one channel you really control. A monthly newsletter with useful, friendly content keeps a brand on the customer's radar without shouting. The brands I admire send genuinely good emails. The boring ones still send weekly discount codes that nobody opens.

Then there's the magic of community. A loyalty programme says “spend ten more, get 5% off.” A community says “you belong here.” Real communities — small Discord servers, a brilliant newsletter with comments, a yearly meet-up — turn customers into a tribe. They forgive small mistakes. They recommend you for free. They become unpaid brand ambassadors. You cannot fake this. Communities know within minutes if the brand is genuine or pretending.

Modern CRM tools — HubSpot, Klaviyo, Mailchimp — help you track, segment and remember every customer. Used kindly, they let a brand send the right message at the right time: a happy birthday note, a useful guide a week after purchase, a check-in after silence. Used badly, they feel creepy and cold. Tools don't create care; they only scale it.

If you take one idea from this column, take this. Spend less time chasing new strangers and more time deserving the customers you already have. Listen. Reply. Remember names. Send useful things. Apologise sincerely when you mess up. The brands that will quietly win the next decade aren't the loudest. They are the ones that act, every day, like a small business that genuinely knows your name.
