📖Reading text

An honest look at how money has changed in the last 15 years — and what it means for your wallet (which, by the way, you barely use anymore). Target language is in amber bold.

"Where Did All the Cash Go?"

★ Money & Tech · 4-minute read

Think about the last time you held a banknote in your hand. For some people reading this, it might have been months ago. For my grandmother, it was this morning, when she withdrew cash from the ATM, like she has every Friday since 1973. For my nephew, who's 16, it was probably never. Welcome to the strange new world of modern banking, where cash is becoming as old-fashioned as the cassette tape — and where new dangers are creeping in through the back door.

Sweden is leading the cashless revolution. Less than 1% of payments there are now made in cash, and many shops, cafés and even churches simply refuse to accept it. Across Europe, the picture is similar: contactless payments, mobile wallets like Apple Pay and Google Pay, and instant bank-to-bank transfers have transformed how we pay for everything from a morning coffee to a new sofa. You no longer carry money — you carry data. The wallet in your pocket is mostly there for nostalgic reasons.

"Cash is freedom. The day cash disappears, so does the last way to spend money without somebody watching." — privacy researcher

The benefits of going cashless are obvious. Payments are faster — a contactless tap takes less than a second. They're more convenient — no more digging for change at the supermarket. They're safer in some ways — if you lose a card, you can freeze it in seconds; if you lose a wallet full of cash, that money is gone for good. And for businesses, handling cash is expensive: counting it, transporting it, protecting it from theft. Many shops genuinely prefer card-only.

But there's a quieter side to this revolution. Older people, particularly those over 70, often struggle with banking apps and contactless payments. Vulnerable customers who don't have smartphones — or who simply don't trust them — are slowly being pushed out of the financial system. ATMs are disappearing in many small towns. Some people, especially in rural areas, can spend twenty minutes driving just to access their own money. The phrase "cash is freedom" sounds dramatic until you realise that, for many people, it's literally true.

And then, of course, there are the scams. The shift to digital banking has been a goldmine for criminals, and the scale of the problem is staggering. In 2024, Europeans lost over €10 billion to online fraud, and the figure rises every year. Phishing emails trick people into clicking fake bank links. Scammers impersonate bank employees and call elderly customers, persuading them to "move their money to a safe account" — which mysteriously turns out to be the scammer's. Romance scammers spend months building fake online relationships before asking for "emergency" loans. And then there's the rise of deepfake voice scams — where AI is used to clone a son's or daughter's voice and beg the parent for help.

Younger people often assume they're safe — but the data tells a different story. While the elderly lose more per scam, young adults aged 20–30 actually fall victim more often, mostly through fake online shops, fake job offers and dodgy investment schemes. Nobody is immune. Anyone can be caught off guard — especially when the message looks like it really did come from your bank.

So how do you protect yourself? The rules are surprisingly simple. Never click a link in an unexpected email — open your bank app or website directly. Never give a bank employee your full password or PIN; no real bank will ever ask. If something feels too good to be true — a job that pays €5,000 a month for two hours' work, an investment that promises 20% returns — it almost certainly is. Take five minutes. Call a friend. Call your bank's official number from their card. Scammers thrive on urgency; slowing down is your single best defence.

Modern banking is, in many ways, a miracle. You can send €1,000 to someone in Brazil before your coffee gets cold. You can split a restaurant bill seven ways with one tap. You can run a whole business from a phone. But every miracle has a cost. The cost here is that you have to be a bit more careful, a bit more sceptical, and a bit more aware of the new ways money can be quietly taken from you.

As one fraud expert recently put it: "Twenty years ago, a thief had to break into your house. Now they just need your phone to ring at the right moment." The future of money is fast, frictionless and digital. Just make sure you stay one step ahead of the people who'd love to take some of yours.

⚠️The 8 most common scams in 2026 — know them, dodge them

Read each scam card carefully. Have you, or anyone you know, ever experienced one of these? We'll come back to your stories in the speaking section.

📧
PHISHING
The Fake Bank Email
An email "from your bank" claims your account is frozen / suspicious. It asks you to click a link and confirm your details. The link goes to a fake site that steals your password.
🛡️ Defence: Real banks NEVER ask for your full password by email. Always log in via the official app.
📞
VISHING — VOICE PHISHING
The "Move Your Money" Call
A caller pretends to be from the fraud department of your bank. "Your money is in danger! Move it to this safe account immediately!" The "safe account" is theirs.
🛡️ Defence: Hang up. Call your bank's official number from the back of your card.
💌
ROMANCE SCAM
The Dating App "Crisis"
After weeks or months of online conversation with a "perfect" stranger, they suddenly need money — for a medical emergency, plane ticket, or stuck shipment. They never video call.
🛡️ Defence: If they avoid video calls and ask for money, it's a scam. Always.
🛍️
FAKE ONLINE SHOP
The Too-Cheap Sneakers
A flashy Instagram or TikTok ad sells designer items at 70% off. The website looks real. You pay — and either receive nothing or a cheap fake.
🛡️ Defence: Search the brand + "scam" before paying. Check reviews on Trustpilot.
📱
SMS / SMISHING
"Your Parcel Couldn't Be Delivered"
A text from "DHL" or "Post Office" says you owe €1.99 to release your parcel. The link asks for your card details. There is no parcel.
🛡️ Defence: If you're not expecting a parcel, ignore the message. Never click the link.
💼
FAKE JOB OFFER
"€500 a Day From Home!"
An "easy" remote job that requires you to pay for "training", "equipment", or to receive money on behalf of strangers. You become a money mule for criminals.
🛡️ Defence: Real jobs never ask you to pay anything upfront, ever.
💰
INVESTMENT SCAM
The "Guaranteed 20% Return"
Slick adverts, fake celebrity endorsements, glossy crypto platforms promising huge returns. Your money disappears, often with the website.
🛡️ Defence: Real investments are never "guaranteed". If it sounds too good to be true, it is.
🎙️
DEEPFAKE / AI SCAM
"Mum, It's Me — I'm in Trouble"
AI clones a relative's voice from social media. The "child" calls in tears, claiming to need urgent money for an accident or arrest. Many parents transfer thousands before realising.
🛡️ Defence: Always verify by calling them back on their normal number. Set a "family password" for emergencies.

📝Grammar: Phrasal Verbs & Conditionals

Two B2 grammar areas you genuinely need for this topic: money phrasal verbs (which appear in every banking conversation) and conditionals (essential for "if I were scammed…" situations and giving advice).

💳 Part 1 — Money phrasal verbs

  • to take out = to borrow / withdraw. I took out a loan. / I took out €50.
  • to pay back = to return borrowed money. I'll pay you back on Friday.
  • to pay off = to finish paying a debt. It took 5 years to pay off the loan.
  • to put aside / put away = to save for later. She puts €100 aside every month.
  • to save up (for) = to gradually save money. We're saving up for a house.
  • to splash out (on) = to spend extravagantly. He splashed out on a new watch.
  • to run out (of money) = to have no more left. I always run out of cash by the 25th.
  • to fork out = to pay reluctantly. I had to fork out €200 for a taxi.
  • to fall for (a scam) = to be tricked by it. Don't fall for that email.
  • to rip (someone) off = to cheat them. That shop totally ripped me off.

💳 Part 2 — Conditionals (1st, 2nd, 3rd)

  • 1st conditional — real future. If you click the link, your account will be hacked.
  • 2nd conditional — hypothetical present. If I won the lottery, I would pay off my mortgage.
  • 3rd conditional — past regret. If I had checked the URL, I wouldn't have fallen for it.
  • Mixing: past condition → present result. If I had saved more, I'd be richer now.
1st: If + present, will + verb (real future)
2nd: If + past, would + verb (unreal present)
3rd: If + past perfect, would have + past participle (past regret)
