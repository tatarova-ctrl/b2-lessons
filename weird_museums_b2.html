Weird Museums

📖Reading text

A travel writer takes us on a tour of the strangest, most surprising museums in the world. Target language is in mustard bold.

"There's a Museum for That? A Tour of the World's Weirdest Collections"

★ Travel column · 4-minute read

The first time I heard there was a museum dedicated entirely to broken relationships, I assumed it was a joke. The second time, I assumed it was art-school nonsense. The third time, I bought a plane ticket. The Museum of Broken Relationships in Zagreb, Croatia, turned out to be one of the most moving experiences of my entire travel life — and the start of a strange obsession. Once you start looking, the world is full of museums that are not, well, the Louvre.

Forget Renaissance paintings and ancient pottery for a moment. There is a museum, somewhere, dedicated to almost anything you can imagine — and several things you really can't. Toilets. Eyeballs. Failed inventions. Instant noodles. Bad art. Behold the world of the weird museum, where someone, somewhere, has built an entire institution around their unusual passion. And honestly? They're often the most memorable places you'll ever set foot in.

"A museum doesn't need a Picasso to be unforgettable. Sometimes it just needs an old toaster and a really good story." — museum curator, Stockholm

Take the Cup Noodles Museum in Yokohama, Japan. It tells the surprisingly emotional story of how Momofuku Ando invented instant noodles in 1958 — apparently after surviving extreme post-war food shortages. Visitors can design their own custom cup, choose ingredients, and watch the noodles being sealed before their eyes. It sounds ridiculous on paper, but the place is packed with families, foodies and curious tourists, and you leave with a genuine appreciation for one of the most useful inventions of the 20th century.

Then there's the Museum of Bad Art in Massachusetts, USA — a museum that proudly displays artworks too bad to ignore. The collection started by accident when one of the founders rescued a painting from a rubbish bin in the 1990s. Today, the museum holds over 700 pieces, all curated with the same affectionate, tongue-in-cheek seriousness as the world's great galleries. Each painting comes with a polite, formal description, often making the piece even funnier. It's a love letter to creative failure — and a reminder that not every artist needs to be Van Gogh to deserve a wall.

Iceland's Phallological Museum in Reykjavík takes the prize for conversation-starting awkwardness. With a collection of more than 200 mammal specimens, this is — to put it diplomatically — exactly what you think it is. It became internationally famous after a documentary, and now sees thousands of bemused visitors a year. There's even a gift shop, which is something to think about.

Tokyo, naturally, has a Parasitological Museum — yes, an entire museum about parasites. The 8.8-metre tapeworm on display is, depending on your sensibility, either fascinating or stomach-churning. A surprising number of dating couples visit. Don't ask me why.

Some weird museums are weird precisely because their topic is so ordinary. The Spam Museum in Minnesota celebrates the famous tinned meat. Sweden has a Hair Museum. Paris has a Sewer Museum. Berlin has a Currywurst Museum (the city's beloved street-food sausage). And Korea has a Toilet Museum, dedicated to raising awareness of sanitation issues worldwide — a serious mission disguised as a deeply silly building shaped like a giant toilet.

What unites all these places? In a word, passion. Someone, somewhere, looked at a topic everyone else dismissed and said: this matters. This deserves a building, a curator, a gift shop. And in their hands, the most ordinary topic suddenly becomes genuinely interesting.

Of course, weird museums aren't for everyone. You won't find blockbuster crowds at most of them. They tend to be small, slightly eccentric, and run on shoestring budgets. The lighting is often poor, the descriptions occasionally amateur, and the gift shops sometimes baffling. But what they offer is something the big museums often can't: a real, warm, very human point of view. A glimpse into one person's strange and wonderful obsession.

If I had to give one piece of travel advice, it would be this: next time you're in a new city, skip the obvious. Skip the famous gallery you'll forget five minutes after leaving. Find the weirdest, smallest, oddest museum you can — and go. You'll walk away with a story you'll be telling for years. As one museum-goer beautifully put it: "The Louvre teaches you about humanity. The Museum of Broken Relationships teaches you about being human."

📝Grammar: Relative Clauses & Descriptive Adjectives

Two grammar areas that are gold for describing places: relative clauses (the museum which… / the curator who…) and descriptive adjectives with the right collocations.

🏛 Part 1 — Relative clauses

  • who = for people. The curator who designed the exhibition is here.
  • which / that = for things. A museum which is dedicated to instant noodles.
  • where = for places. It's a city where they have a sewer museum.
  • whose = possession. The museum, whose collection is enormous, opens at 9.
  • when = for times. 1958 was the year when instant noodles were invented.
  • Defining clauses — no commas, give essential info. The museum that I told you about was incredible.
  • Non-defining clauses — with commas, give extra info. The Louvre, which is in Paris, is the world's most-visited museum.
Defining (no commas): The man who built the museum is Japanese.
Non-defining (with commas): Mr Ando, who built the museum, is now famous.

🏛 Part 2 — Adjectives + collocations for describing places

  • Order of adjectives: opinion → size → age → shape → colour → origin → material → purpose. A wonderful, small, old wooden Italian museum.
  • Strong adjectives (no "very"): amazing, fantastic, incredible, fascinating, unforgettable, breathtaking, hilarious
  • Adverbs that go with strong adjectives: absolutely / utterly / completely + amazing/incredible/unforgettable
  • Common collocations: a quirky little museum, a packed gallery, a moving exhibition, a vast collection, a stunning display
