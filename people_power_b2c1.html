🎯Headhunting & Recruitment

A senior headhunter on what really gets you hired — and what quietly gets you rejected. Target idioms are in gold.

The CV Got You In. The First Five Minutes Will Keep You There.

★ Hannah Lindqvist · Executive headhunter · Twelve years placing senior talent

After twelve years interviewing senior candidates, I'll let you in on something nobody likes to admit: most hires aren't decided by the answers. They're decided in the first five minutes — before anyone has said anything that matters. We tell ourselves we're being objective. We're not. We're pattern-matching.

The CV gets you through the door. Beyond that, it barely matters. What I'm actually watching for is harder to define and easier to feel. Do you ask questions back? Do you smile at the receptionist as well as the CEO? When I challenge you, do you defend or do you think? That last one tells me more than any qualification on your résumé.

Most candidates lose the role in small, accidental ways. They bad-mouth their last boss — one of the biggest red flags I see. They describe themselves as a “perfectionist” as if it's a brave confession. They talk endlessly about their needs and not once about how they'd actually help the team. None of these are dealbreakers on paper. All of them are dealbreakers in the room.

Nobody is ever hired for being the smartest person available. They're hired for being the easiest person to work with who is also good enough.

The market itself has changed. Today's senior roles are filled in a quiet war for talent: the best people are rarely looking, they're already employed, and they need to be approached, not posted to. That's why we spend most of our time chasing passive candidates on LinkedIn, not screening eager applicants. The active candidate pile, frankly, is the last place I look.

When you do get the offer, here's where it gets uncomfortable. If you're any good, your current employer will hit you with a counter-offer within hours. Tempting, yes. But the data is brutal: roughly half of people who accept a counter-offer leave within twelve months anyway. The reasons that pushed you out don't disappear just because the salary went up.

And once you do start a new role, the first ninety days are everything. The people I see succeed don't try to redesign the company in week one. They ask questions, build relationships, and hit the ground running on small, visible wins. They're a culture fit not because they agree with everyone, but because they bother to understand the room before changing it. So before your next interview, stop rehearsing answers. Decide instead what kind of colleague you want to be — and walk in as that person from minute one. People hire other people. Not bullet points.

📅Meetings & Meeting Culture

A productivity coach on why your meetings are killing your week. Target idioms are in gold.

The Meeting That Should Have Been an Email

★ Daniel Ortiz · Productivity coach · Inside 200 companies a year

I'm going to say what most professionals quietly think and rarely say out loud: about half of your meetings shouldn't exist. They eat the day, leak energy, and reach decisions that could have been written down in three sentences. We're so used to death by meeting that we've forgotten what working actually looks like.

Here's the diagnostic I use with executive teams. For every meeting in your calendar this week, ask one question: What decision will be made in this meeting that couldn't be made by email? If you can't answer it in one sentence, it's an email. If you can, it deserves the time. Most people, when they try this honestly, cut their meeting load by a third in a fortnight.

A meeting without a decision is a status update with snacks. Status updates belong in writing.

The well-run meetings I see share three boring habits. They start with a written agenda nobody is allowed to add to mid-flight. They have a clear chair whose job is to steer the conversation — not to dominate it, but to gently bring people back when they go off on a tangent or derail the discussion. And they end with action points: who is doing what, by when. No action points, no meeting.

On the etiquette side, the biggest sin is talking over people. I've watched million-dollar decisions get made by whoever was loudest in the room and not by whoever was right. Strong chairs name it instantly: “Let Priya finish.” Two words. They change the whole room.

Then there's the modern crisis: back-to-back meetings with no thinking time between them. We schedule humans like CPUs and then act surprised when nothing creative comes out. The healthiest companies I work with are quietly shifting work to async — written updates and shared documents — so the rare live meeting is actually about deciding something hard together. Meetings hide chaos. Writing forces clarity.

Finally, please learn to wrap up. Most meetings overrun because nobody knows how to end them. A good chair pulls the plug at minus three: “We have three minutes. Action points, decisions, who does what.” That's it. Anything else can be a follow-up. If you take one habit from this column, take that one. Your week will silently get its life back.

📚Skilling & Upskilling

A learning lead on why the half-life of a skill is now five years — and what to do about it. Target idioms are in gold.

What Got You Here Won't Get You There

★ Anya Saito · Head of Learning · Built upskilling programmes across three industries

There's a number I share with every CEO I work with that quietly changes their plans for the year: the average commercial skill now has a shelf-life of about five years. The thing your team learned in 2020 isn't broken; it's just running out of road. The companies that keep winning don't pay for more degrees. They build the habit of relentless, low-key upskilling into how people work.

The first myth I dismantle is that upskilling means abandoning what you know. It doesn't. To upskill is to go deeper in the field you're already in. Reskilling — learning an entirely different job — is the dramatic story HR conferences love, but the truth is that most people don't need to reinvent themselves. They need to sharpen the edges of what they're already doing.

The most dangerous career mistake isn't picking the wrong skill. It's quietly switching to autopilot in the right one.

Once you've been good at something for a decade, the brain stops working very hard. That's why the most experienced people on a team are sometimes the slowest to grow. They're not lazy — they're efficient, which looks identical from the outside. The cure is uncomfortable: deliberately put yourself on a learning curve every year. A new tool, a new method, a new domain at the edge of your job. Discomfort is your only honest feedback that growth is happening.

Then there's the part nobody likes: identifying your skills gap. Most people guess it. The strong ones ask. Send three colleagues you respect a single question: “What could I do, that I currently don't, that would actually be useful?” Then prepare yourself: their answers will be sharper than you expect. That's the point.

The format matters less than people think. Bootcamps, micro-credentials, online courses, books, a mentor, an AI tutor — pick whatever you'll actually finish. Eighty percent of a finished course beats one hundred percent of a course you signed up for and abandoned in week two.

If you want one rule to live by, here it is. Aim to be slightly uncomfortable for an hour every week with something professionally new. Not heroic. Not glamorous. Just one steady hour. That single habit will compound into a career most of your peers will quietly envy. Lifelong learning isn't a slogan. It's the only competitive advantage that doesn't depreciate.

💬Customer Service & Relationships

A CX lead on why most companies measure the wrong thing — and how to actually keep customers. Target idioms are in gold.

Your Customers Will Forgive Almost Anything Except This

★ Liam Murphy · Customer Experience director · Lifted retention at three global brands

After fifteen years inside customer-service teams, I can sum up what really keeps customers loyal in one short sentence: people forgive almost any mistake, but they will not forgive being made to feel stupid or invisible. Everything else is detail. Once you understand that, half of customer experience stops being mysterious.

Most companies measure the wrong thing. They obsess over NPS — the famous “how likely are you to recommend us” score — and treat a 7 like a personal insult. NPS isn't useless, but it's a thermometer, not a treatment. Far more useful is to follow customers through the entire customer journey and ask, at every touchpoint, one specific question: was this moment easier or harder than the customer expected?

Loyalty is built in the small moments where you didn't have to try, and you did anyway.

Here's the part executives don't want to hear. The single biggest driver of churn isn't price. It's effort. Customers leave the brand that made cancelling a subscription feel like a hostage negotiation. They stay with the one that fixed the broken order in two messages without making them prove anything. Friction is the silent killer of every brand — the slow tax of every minor annoyance you've stopped noticing.

On the people side, the best customer-service teams I've ever run weren't the ones with the perfect scripts. They were the ones whose agents had real authority to fix things. Empowered humans build rapport in 30 seconds and solve in two. Disempowered humans read approved sentences and infuriate everyone. If your agents have to escalate every refund, your brand is leaking trust whether you can see it or not.

Of course, plenty of small touches matter too. A genuinely warm welcome. Remembering a customer's name. An apology before a fix, never instead of one. The occasional going the extra mile — a small gesture nobody asked for — turns ordinary buyers into brand ambassadors who do your marketing for free.

Then there's the long game. Loyalty programmes are fine; community is better. The brands quietly winning the next decade aren't optimising discount codes — they're building tribes that genuinely care about the product and each other. So before your next CX upgrade, walk your own customer journey, end to end, as a stranger would. You'll find five painful pain points in an afternoon. Fix three of them. Your retention numbers will quietly transform — without spending a cent on advertising.
