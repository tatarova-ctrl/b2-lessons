Data Centres Popping Up
as Mushrooms After Rain

The language of the global data centre boom — why they're being built everywhere, who's building them, and what the rush really means for energy, land, and society.

📖 Reading text

A data centre strategy consultant shares what's really driving the global boom — and why the consequences reach far beyond the server room. Target language is in orange bold.

MO
★ Marcus Osei, 41 · Senior Data Centre Strategy Consultant
14 years in the industry · Based in London · Works across Europe, the Middle East and Asia

There's a phrase I keep hearing from journalists and planners right now: data centres are popping up like mushrooms after rain. And honestly, it's not an exaggeration. In the past three years, planning applications for large-scale hyperscale facilities have hit record levels across Europe, the US, and Southeast Asia. The AI boom, the explosion in cloud computing, and the relentless growth of streaming, e-commerce, and digital workloads have created a demand for compute power that the industry is genuinely struggling to keep pace with.

What most people don't realise is that a data centre isn't just a big room full of computers. It's an enormously complex piece of critical infrastructure — as essential to modern life as a power station or a water treatment plant. Inside, you'll find thousands of server racks, intricate cooling systems to stop the hardware from overheating, and redundant power supplies designed to keep everything running even if the national grid goes down. The best facilities have a Power Usage Effectiveness rating close to 1.0 — meaning almost none of the energy they consume is wasted on anything other than actual computing.

"Every time someone says 'the cloud', what they really mean is someone else's computer in a warehouse the size of an aircraft hangar, somewhere outside Dublin or Frankfurt."

So who's building all of these? Primarily the hyperscalers — Amazon Web Services, Microsoft Azure, and Google Cloud — who are pouring tens of billions of dollars into new campuses every year. But there's also a fast-growing colocation market, where companies like Equinix and Digital Realty rent out space to businesses that want to house their own servers without managing the building themselves. And then there are the edge data centres — smaller, faster facilities built closer to end users to reduce latency, the tiny but critical delay between sending a request and getting a response. You can't run a self-driving car on a connection that takes half a second to respond.

The problem — and it's a serious one — is that all of this comes with a staggering environmental cost. A single large data centre can consume as much electricity as a small city. Water consumption is another growing concern; many facilities use millions of litres per day in evaporative cooling systems. Campaigners argue that the industry is greenwashing — making loud claims about renewable energy targets while quietly drawing on fossil-fuel-heavy local grids. The debate is fierce, and it's far from settled.

Governments are caught between two forces. On one hand, they want the jobs, the investment, and the geopolitical prestige that comes with hosting major digital infrastructure. On the other, they're facing legitimate pressure from local communities about land use, energy strain, and water. Ireland, for example, briefly imposed a moratorium on new data centre connections in Dublin because the existing electricity grid simply couldn't cope with the load. That's not a niche problem — it's a sign of things to come globally.

What I find fascinating — and what the media often misses — is that this isn't just a technology story. It's a story about sovereign data, about which countries get to control where the world's information sits, and who has access to it. When a European company hosts its data on a US hyperscaler, there are genuine legal and security questions about data sovereignty. That's exactly why countries like Germany, France, and Saudi Arabia are investing heavily in nationally-controlled alternatives.

If I had to cut through the noise, I'd say this: data centres are the infrastructure of the 21st century, the same way railways were in the 19th. The question isn't whether to build them. It's whether we're building them smartly, sustainably, and in a way that distributes their benefits — and their costs — fairly.

📝 Grammar: Conditionals & Reported Speech

A quick refresher, then a mixed exercise. Some items need a conditional, some need reported speech, and some need both. All sentences use data centre contexts.

◆ Conditionals — the quick map

1st conditional — real futureIf the grid can't cope with the load, the council will reject the planning application.
2nd conditional — hypothetical presentIf I were the minister, I would impose stricter water-use limits.
3rd conditional — past regretIf they had invested in renewables earlier, they wouldn't have faced the backlash.
Mixed — past condition → present resultIf Ireland had built more grid capacity, it wouldn't be rationing connections now.
2nd: If + past simple, would + infinitive  ·  3rd: If + past perfect, would have + past participle

◆ Reported speech — key shifts

Present simple → past simple"Latency is a critical issue." → She said latency was a critical issue.
Present continuous → past continuous"We are expanding our campus." → He said they were expanding their campus.
Past simple → past perfect"You ignored the energy report." → She said I had ignored the energy report.
will → would"We'll reach net zero by 2030." → They said they would reach net zero by 2030.
Commands"Reduce your cooling water usage." → She told them to reduce their cooling water usage.
Questions (indirect)"Why didn't you flag the grid issue?" → He asked why I hadn't flagged the grid issue.

📝 Grammar 2: Modal Verbs for Obligation, Ability & Deduction

Modals are essential for discussing policies, technical possibilities, and conclusions — all central to data centre debates. Learn the key contrasts, then practise across three exercise types.

◆ Modal verbs — the essential map

must / have toStrong obligation — All facilities must meet minimum PUE standards.
should / ought toAdvice or moral expectation — Operators should publish their water usage data.
can / be able toAbility or possibility — Edge data centres can reduce latency significantly.
could (past ability)Past possibility — Early data centres could not run without massive cooling.
may / mightPresent or future uncertainty — The moratorium might be lifted next year.
must (deduction)Logical certainty — Consumption must be enormous if it equals a small city.
can't (negative deduction)Logical impossibility — Those claims can't be accurate — the numbers don't add up.
should have / could havePast criticism or regret — They should have invested in renewables sooner.
