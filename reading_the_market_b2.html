📈 Line Graphs: Oil Prices

Reading a line graph and describing rises, falls, spikes and long-term trends. Highlighted items are market-trend collocations.

Oil: The Roller-Coaster of the Modern Economy

★ Daniel Park · Energy market analyst · Reads charts for a living

The line graph below shows the average price of Brent crude oil in US dollars per barrel from 2020 to 2026. In seven years, the market has done almost everything a market can do: collapse, rebound, spike, level off and quietly drift downwards again. Learn to describe this graph well, and you can describe almost any market.

Let's break the trend down. In 2020, the price bottomed out at around $42 as the pandemic caused demand to collapse. Almost nobody was flying, driving or commuting, and stored oil piled up so quickly that some traders briefly paid customers to take it away. This was a genuine crisis for producers.

“A single line on a chart usually hides several years of confusion, panic and quiet correction. Learn to read the line and you'll never listen to news the same way again.”

Then came the recovery. In 2021, as economies picked up, demand bounced back and the price climbed steadily to about $71. This is a classic rebound. The market didn't just recover; it overshot, as producers had cut supply during 2020 and couldn't ramp production back up fast enough.

In 2022, the price skyrocketed to about $99. This was the Russia–Ukraine shock: fears about supply drove prices up sharply, and speculators piled into the market. This is called a price spike — a short, dramatic move driven by fear, not by fundamentals. Spikes usually don't last.

By 2023, the price eased off to $82 as supply concerns died down and demand growth slowed. In 2024, it hovered around $80. This is a plateau: a period when a price levels off and doesn't move much in either direction. Analysts hate plateaus because there's nothing to talk about.

Then, in 2025 and 2026, the price began to drift down gently to $74 and then $69. This isn't a crash; it's a gradual decline. New supply from the US, slower Chinese growth, and rising electric-vehicle sales weighed on the market. Nothing dramatic, just steady downward pressure.

There's a real vocabulary for describing charts like this, and B2 students should learn it. Prices rise sharply, climb steadily, edge up, or surge. They also fall sharply, slide gently, drop back, or plunge. They can fluctuate (go up and down), stagnate (stay flat despite pressure), or consolidate (settle in a narrow range). Use the right word and you sound like an analyst; use the wrong one and you sound like someone reading the label wrong.

There's also the language of comparison. Oil is up 64% from its 2020 low, but down 30% from its 2022 peak. Prices are higher year-on-year or lower quarter-on-quarter. When you describe a chart properly, always give the timeframe and, if possible, the peak and the trough.

If you take one idea, take this. Charts don't tell you what will happen next. They tell you what already happened. Learn the words for describing that clearly, and you'll be halfway to reading any market in the world. The other half is knowing when the story on the news is really the story on the chart.

📊 Bar Charts: Global Chip Sales

Reading a bar chart and comparing quantities across categories. Targets are in gold.

The Chip War in One Bar Chart

★ Yara Hassan · Tech industry analyst · Follows global semiconductors

You cannot understand modern geopolitics without understanding one industry: semiconductors. Chips power everything — your phone, your car, your fridge, your fighter jet. The bar chart below shows approximate global semiconductor sales by region in 2025, in US dollars billion. Read it carefully; it explains why governments spend billions on chip factories.

Let's break the numbers down. The US comfortably tops the chart at $195 billion. American firms design most of the world's most advanced chips (Nvidia, AMD, Intel, Qualcomm), even when the physical manufacturing happens elsewhere. The US leads the market by a clear margin.

“The country that controls chip design and chip manufacturing controls the next decade. Everything else follows.”

China comes second at $165 billion. China buys almost every chip it can, and produces a huge share of the world's simpler chips (memory, sensors, older-generation processors). China has been catching up aggressively, though export controls from the US have held it back in the most advanced categories.

South Korea is third at $130 billion. This may surprise students who don't follow the industry. Two companies — Samsung and SK Hynix — account for most of the world's memory chips. If your phone relies on DRAM or NAND, it probably came from Korea. Korea punches well above its weight here.

Taiwan ranks fourth at $90 billion. This looks modest, but Taiwan quietly controls the most important chip factory on earth: TSMC. Most of the world's cutting-edge chips — the ones inside iPhones, AI servers, high-end laptops — are actually made in Taiwan. Its bar is small; its strategic weight is enormous.

Europe ($55bn) and Japan ($50bn) are quietly lagging behind the leaders. Europe has world-class chip equipment makers (ASML in the Netherlands) but relatively few large chip makers. Japan was once dominant here in the 1980s; it has slowly lost ground, but is now investing heavily to catch up. Both regions are pouring subsidies into new factories.

There's a real vocabulary for bar charts. One category can lead, dominate, top the ranking, come out on top, or be head and shoulders above the rest. Others trail behind, fall short of the leader, lag, or bring up the rear. Two bars can be roughly equal, neck and neck, or close together. A tall bar towers over the smaller ones.

There's also the language of proportion. The US accounts for about 33% of global chip sales. China makes up around 28%. Together, these two account for over 60% of the world market. You can also say one region is three times the size of another, or that Japan is roughly the same size as Europe.

There's the language of change too, even in a static snapshot. “China has grown its share from 8% in 2010 to 24% today.” “Taiwan's revenue has doubled since 2018.” “Europe's share has been shrinking for a decade.” Bar charts often invite comparisons across time even when the chart itself shows one year.

If you take one idea, take this. Never just say “this bar is bigger.” Give the value. Give the ranking. Compare to a rival. Note whether a category is growing or shrinking. That's how analysts read bar charts, and it's how you should describe them too. The US leads for now. The story of the next decade is whether China really closes the gap.

📉 Two-Line Comparison: EV vs Diesel

Two lines going in opposite directions — the language of market shifts. Targets are in gold.

Diesel Is Losing, EVs Are Winning

★ Marco Vidal · Auto industry journalist · Watches European new-car sales

This chart tells one of the clearest stories in modern industry: the quiet, steady death of diesel and the equally steady rise of electric vehicles in the European new-car market. Two lines. One going up. One going down. The whole automotive world is in that picture.

Let's walk through the numbers. In 2020, diesel still accounted for 28% of new-car sales in Europe. EVs were an emerging category at just 11%. Diesel was familiar; EVs were niche. Fast-forward six years, and the picture has quietly flipped. In 2026, EVs hit 37% of new-car sales. Diesel fell to just 8%.

“Nobody watching the news in 2020 would have believed that diesel would collapse this fast, or that EVs would this quickly become the dominant new-car story.”

This is a beautiful example of two lines with opposite trends. EVs have climbed steadily, gained market share year after year, and are now taking off. Diesel has lost ground every single year, quietly tailing off until it's almost a niche. When you describe such charts, use verbs that capture direction: EVs are on the rise, diesel is on the way out. Prices are heading up; volumes are heading down. Trends are picking up or tailing off.

Why has this happened? Three forces. First, city bans: many European cities have phased out old diesel vehicles from their centres. Second, EV incentives: governments have rolled out subsidies, tax breaks and cheap charging. Third, technology: EV batteries have come down in price and range has improved dramatically. These three forces combined to make one line go up sharply and the other slump.

There's also the language of crossovers. In 2020, the lines were far apart (diesel much higher). By 2022, the gap was closing. By 2023, they nearly crossed. From 2024 onward, EVs were clearly outperforming diesel. When describing charts, look for the turning point — the moment when trends genuinely change direction or when one line overtakes another. The turning point is where the story lives.

There's also the language of momentum. “The EV trend is gathering pace.” “Diesel is in terminal decline.” “The gap has widened every year.” “Momentum is building behind hybrids.” Momentum verbs are perfect for describing markets in transition.

There's also the vocabulary of market share. Products can corner a market, dominate, edge out rivals, or be squeezed out. Some are gaining ground, others losing ground. Some brands hold their own, others fall by the wayside. Some technologies are ahead of the curve; others are behind the times.

For B2 speakers, describing two-line charts is a genuinely valuable skill in interviews, presentations, and journalism. The trick is to first describe each line separately, then compare them. Use words like “while”, “in contrast”, “by comparison”, and “on the other hand”. Never just list numbers. Tell the story the two lines together are telling.

If you take one idea, take this. When two lines cross, something important is happening in the world. Learn to spot the crossover point, describe the momentum around it, and explain the forces driving it. Do that clearly, and you'll sound like a market analyst even when you're not. The end of diesel isn't just a chart. It's a real industry story, unfolding in front of everyone who knows how to read the lines.

🥧 Pie Charts: World Energy Mix

Reading a pie chart and describing composition, shares and long-term shifts. Targets are in gold.

Where Does the World's Energy Actually Come From?

★ Elena Marchetti · Energy policy analyst · Advises governments on the transition

Ask most people where the world's energy comes from, and you'll get very different answers. Some think it's mostly renewables now. Some think it's still mostly oil. Some have no idea. The pie chart below shows the approximate share of primary world energy from each source in 2026. The truth surprises almost everyone.

Let's break the chart down. Oil still makes up around 30% of world primary energy — the single biggest slice. Coal accounts for another 24%. Gas takes up 22%. Together, these three fossil fuels represent the lion's share — roughly 76% — of world energy. Yes, in 2026, more than three quarters of world energy still comes from fossil fuels. This is genuinely uncomfortable and important to know.

“The world's energy mix is like a giant ship: even with everyone rowing in the same direction, it turns very slowly. That's not failure. It's physics.”

Renewables — solar, wind, biomass — make up around 14%. This is a big jump from the 6% a decade ago. Solar and wind have been scaling up at extraordinary speed. But they still account for a modest slice of the total. Nuclear and hydro each hold about 5%. Together, low-carbon sources represent roughly 24% of world primary energy. That's the number to remember.

Reading pie charts uses different vocabulary from line graphs. Slices can be the biggest chunk, a significant share, a modest portion, or a small slice. Categories can make up or account for a percentage. Two slices together add up to a larger share. Sources take up different proportions. When one slice is much bigger than the others, we say it dominates the mix or represents the lion's share.

There's also the language of shift. Even in a static pie chart, we can compare with the past: “Renewables have doubled in a decade.” “Coal's share has fallen from 28% to 24%.” “Gas has grown from 19% to 22%.” “Oil has held roughly steady.” These are ways to bring a static chart to life.

Then there's the language of policy and transition. The world is shifting away from coal. Governments are phasing in renewables. Countries are rolling out solar and wind at record pace. Some are doubling down on nuclear. Others are investing heavily in gas as a bridge fuel. All these verbs help describe the political story behind the chart.

The reality is uncomfortable. Fossil fuels still dominate, and even in an optimistic scenario, they'll account for around half of world energy by 2035. The transition is happening, but slowly. The chart also hides big regional differences: Norway runs almost entirely on hydro and wind; India still depends heavily on coal. A global pie chart is a rough average of very different national ones.

For B2 students, pie charts are common in Cambridge exams, IELTS Writing Task 1, and business presentations. The trick is to always give three things: the biggest slice, the smallest slice, and the most surprising slice. Then compare, and then note any change over time if you can.

There's also a bigger picture to hold onto. Even at 14%, renewables are growing faster than any other category. Solar in particular is doubling roughly every three years. If that pace continues, the pie chart could look genuinely different by 2040. That's the real story: not the current shares, but where each slice is heading.

If you take one idea, take this. Fossil fuels still make up three quarters of world energy. Renewables are rising fast but still small. Nuclear and hydro are steady. Describe pie charts by naming the biggest slice, the smallest slice, and the biggest surprise. Then explain what's shifting. That's how analysts read composition data, and it's how you should describe them too.
