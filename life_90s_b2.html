📖Reading text

A writer remembers what daily life was actually like in the '90s — and asks whether the "good old days" were really that good. Target language is in yellow bold.

"The Good Old Days — Were They Really That Good?"

★ Nostalgia column · 4-minute read

Recently I asked my 14-year-old nephew if he'd ever heard of a Walkman. He thought I meant a brand of running shoes. When I tried to explain it — a small portable device that played one cassette at a time, no skip button, no shuffle, no playlists, you had to rewind with a pencil when the tape got stuck — he looked at me as if I were describing life on Mars. "And you actually liked this?" he asked. The honest answer is: yes. We didn't know any better, and somehow, that was part of the magic.

The '90s feel like another planet now. There were no smartphones, no social media, no streaming services, no Wi-Fi, and definitely no Google. If you wanted to know something — anything — you either asked someone, looked it up in an actual encyclopedia, or simply didn't find out. People used to argue for hours about who sang a particular song or which actor was in a film, because there was no way to settle the argument. Imagine that.

"In the '90s, boredom was a real feeling. Today, it's almost impossible — and I'm not sure that's a good thing." — sociologist, BBC interview

Music was a completely different ritual. We recorded songs off the radio, finger hovering over the play and record buttons, hoping the DJ wouldn't talk over the intro. We made mixtapes for our friends — and especially for the people we had a crush on. Crafting the perfect mixtape took hours and was, in its own way, a love letter. Today's "Add to Spotify Playlist" feels emotionally poor by comparison.

The internet, in the '90s, was a strange and slow new world. People connected through dial-up — that legendary screeching sound your modem made — and you had to wait, sometimes literally minutes, for a single image to load. If someone in your house picked up the phone, the internet died. Email was magical. Chat rooms were the wild west. The very idea that one day we'd all carry the entire internet in our pockets sounded like science fiction.

Even watching films and television was a different experience. You couldn't just stream what you wanted — you had to physically go to a video rental shop, walk among the shelves, and hope your chosen title hadn't already been taken. Friday nights at Blockbuster were a weekly ritual. And if you missed your favourite TV show on Tuesday at 8 p.m., that was it — you missed it. There were no second chances.

Of course, we shouldn't romanticise the '90s. Plenty of things were worse. Mobile phones, when they finally appeared, were the size of bricks and cost a fortune to use. Maps were paper, getting lost was easy, and you couldn't simply order food on an app at midnight. The world was less convenient, less informed, and in many ways, smaller. People didn't know what they didn't know.

And yet, ask anyone who lived through the '90s, and they'll tell you something was different. Slower. Quieter. More present. People made plans and stuck to them, because once you left the house, there was no way to text "running 10 minutes late". You read books on long train journeys. You looked out of windows. You actually remembered phone numbers. There were entire afternoons where nobody could reach you, and that was completely normal.

What we miss, I think, isn't really the technology — or the lack of it. It's the pace of life. It's the feeling that you weren't always slightly distracted, slightly scrolling, slightly somewhere else. Boredom wasn't yet seen as a problem to solve. Waiting was just part of life. And inside all that waiting, weirdly enough, your imagination got plenty of space to breathe.

So were the '90s really the good old days? Honestly, no — and yes. They were no golden age, but they had a quieter rhythm we've quietly lost. Maybe what we should take from the '90s isn't the fashion or the music or the chunky electronics. Maybe it's the simple, almost forgotten skill of doing one thing at a time. As one writer beautifully put it: "We didn't have everything in the '90s. We just had enough."

📼Then vs Now — what's really changed?

For each topic, look at how things were then and how they are now. Which was better? Discuss with your teacher!

🎵 Music
⏪ THEN — '90s
Mixtapes & Walkmans

You recorded songs off the radio, made mixtapes for friends and crushes, and carried a Walkman with one cassette. CDs were luxury. You didn't skip songs.

▶ NOW
Streaming & algorithms

Spotify, Apple Music, Tidal — instant access to 100 million songs. Algorithms make playlists for you. Music is everywhere and yet feels strangely disposable.

📺 Watching films & TV
⏪ THEN — '90s
Video rental & TV schedules

Blockbuster on Friday night. Choosing among VHS tapes. If you missed your show at 8 p.m., it was gone. Adverts in the middle. Nobody complained.

▶ NOW
Streaming on demand

Netflix, Disney+, HBO. Watch anything, anytime, on any device. Choice paralysis is real — sometimes you spend 40 minutes browsing before watching anything.

📞 Communication
⏪ THEN — '90s
Landlines & letters

Family phone in the hallway. You memorised numbers. You wrote letters that took days to arrive. If someone wasn't home, they were unreachable.

▶ NOW
Smartphones & instant messaging

WhatsApp, video calls, voice notes. You can reach anyone, anywhere, instantly. Reply expectations: 5 minutes. "Why didn't you answer?"

🌐 Internet
⏪ THEN — '90s
Dial-up & encyclopedias

Slow, screechy internet that died when someone picked up the phone. AltaVista. Yahoo! You had to wait minutes for a photo to load.

▶ NOW
Wi-Fi & AI everywhere

5G, Google, AI chatbots that answer in seconds. Fact-checking is instant. The world's knowledge fits in your pocket.

📷 Photos
⏪ THEN — '90s
Disposable cameras & photo shops

24 photos per roll. Wait 3 days for development. No filters. You couldn't see the photo until it was printed. Half were terrible — and that was OK.

▶ NOW
Smartphones & instant filters

Unlimited photos, instant edits, AI enhancement. Everyone is a photographer. We take 50 photos to pick one — and forget the moment itself.

🎮 Hobbies & play
⏪ THEN — '90s
Outside, with friends

Kids played outside until the streetlights came on. Tamagotchis, yo-yos, board games, arcade machines, hide-and-seek. A "smart toy" was a Furby.

▶ NOW
Screens, screens, screens

Online games, YouTube, TikTok. Even toys are now apps. Many kids barely play outside anymore. Nostalgic parents quietly worry.

📝Grammar: Used to / Would / Past Habits

A grammar topic perfectly matched to this lesson — "used to" and "would" are the language we use to talk about past habits and how things were different.

📼 Part 1 — Used to + infinitive

  • Use used to for past habits and past states that are no longer true.
  • Past habit: I used to play outside every day.
  • Past state: There used to be a video shop here.
  • Negative: didn't use to (notice: NO "d"!) I didn't use to like coffee.
  • Question: Did you use to…? Did you use to play with Tamagotchis?
used to + base verb → I used to make mixtapes. There used to be a phone box here.
Negative: didn't use to + base verb (NOT "didn't used to")

📼 Part 2 — Would for past habits

  • Use would for repeated past actions / habits — but NOT for past states.
  • Every Friday, we would rent a video and order pizza. ✓ (action)
  • I would have a Walkman. ✗ (state — use "used to")
  • Use used to for both habits AND states. Use would only for habits.
would + base verb → for repeated past ACTIONS only
used to + base verb → for past actions AND past states

📼 Part 3 — Be used to vs Get used to

  • To be used to + -ing = to be familiar with something. I'm used to working from home now.
  • To get used to + -ing = to become familiar with something. I'm slowly getting used to the new phone.
  • ⚠️ Don't confuse with used to + infinitive (past habit)! Different meanings!
used to do = past habit | be / get used to doing = familiar with
