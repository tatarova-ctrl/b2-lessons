"AI Won't Replace Doctors — But It Will Free Them Up"

★ Dr. Elena Marchetti, 44 · Founder & CEO, NovaMed AI · Milan

When I founded NovaMed AI seven years ago, a lot of people in the industry thought I was chasing a pipe dream. Back then, the idea of using machine learning to interpret medical scans, predict disease or support clinical decisions was still seen as science fiction. Today, our technology is used in over 200 hospitals across Europe, and we've just secured another round of funding. So yes — it turns out the pipe dream was actually a gap in the market waiting to be filled.

People often ask me: "What exactly does your company do?" In simple terms, we build AI tools that help doctors make better decisions faster. Our flagship product analyses radiology images — CT scans, MRIs, X-rays — and flags potential abnormalities that a human eye might miss, especially at the end of a 12-hour shift. We don't replace the radiologist. We give them a second pair of eyes. And in medicine, a second opinion can be the difference between catching a tumour early and catching it too late.

"Elena, aren't you worried AI will take doctors' jobs?" a journalist asked me recently. "Honestly," I said, "I'm much more worried about a shortage of doctors than about an excess of algorithms."

And that's the real story people miss. Europe is facing a chronic shortage of healthcare professionals. Waiting lists are getting longer, clinicians are burning out, and budgets are stretched to breaking point. In this context, AI isn't a luxury — it's a necessity. A well-designed algorithm can process vast amounts of data in seconds and help streamline workflows, reduce administrative burdens, and give doctors back the most precious thing they have: time with their patients. That, to me, is the real promise of AI.

Of course, it's not all sunshine and roses. The challenges are significant, and I'd be naïve to pretend otherwise. Let me be candid about some of them.

✔ The advantages we see daily
  • early detection of serious conditions
  • faster, more accurate diagnoses
  • reducing the workload on overstretched staff
  • personalised treatment based on patient data
  • lower costs over time for public healthcare
✘ The concerns we can't ignore
  • data privacy and GDPR compliance
  • algorithmic bias in training data
  • the "black box" problem — lack of transparency
  • over-reliance on technology by clinicians
  • unequal access between well-funded and underfunded regions

The issue of bias is one I take extremely seriously. If you train an algorithm on flawed data, the algorithm inherits those flaws — and scales them. A famous example is AI models that were trained mostly on data from white male patients. They simply didn't perform as well on women or on patients of colour. That's not a small problem — that's a life-or-death issue. At NovaMed, we've invested heavily in diverse, high-quality datasets, because, as we like to say internally: "garbage in, garbage out."

Then there's the question of trust. A recent survey showed that only 42% of Europeans would feel comfortable with an AI involved in their diagnosis. And honestly? I understand the hesitation. Medicine is personal. It's about human touch, empathy, compassion. No algorithm will ever replace that. Our job, as a company, is to be absolutely transparent about what our tools do, what they don't do, and where the limits lie. Transparency is the foundation of trust, and in this industry, trust is everything.

Regulation is another big topic. The EU's AI Act has been a real game changer. Some people in the tech world complain that Europe over-regulates and that this is why innovation tends to flourish in the US or leapfrog ahead in China. I disagree. Yes, it slows us down — but it also forces us to build responsible, explainable, ethical AI from the ground up. And I'd rather play the long game than rush a product to market that puts patients at risk.

If I had to give one piece of advice to any young founder entering this space, it would be this: stay humble. Healthcare is not like other industries. When software fails in e-commerce, someone doesn't get their parcel. When software fails in healthcare, someone can lose their life. The stakes couldn't be higher, and that's exactly why this work matters so much.

People ask me if I'm optimistic. The honest answer is yes — but it's a cautious optimism. The next ten years will define whether AI in healthcare becomes a genuine tool for equity, or whether it just widens the gap between those who can afford good medicine and those who can't. That's the fight. That's what keeps me up at night. And that's why, even on the hardest days, I still get out of bed excited to come to work.

📝Grammar: Passive Voice & Modals of Speculation

Two grammar areas that really level up B2/C1 writing and speaking — both are everywhere in articles and interviews about AI and healthcare.

⚕ Part 1 — Passive voice

  • We use the passive when the action matters more than who does it. This is extremely common in scientific, medical and tech writing.
  • Form: subject + BE + past participle (+ by + agent)
  • Present: Patient data is stored securely.
  • Past: The system was developed by our team in 2018.
  • Present perfect: Several bugs have been fixed recently.
  • Future: The product will be launched in March.
  • Modal: The algorithm must be trained on diverse data.
Active: Doctors use AI to diagnose. Passive: AI is used (by doctors) to diagnose.

⚕ Part 2 — Modals of speculation (present)

  • must = I'm sure / very likely true. The system must be working — there are no errors.
  • might / may / could = possible / uncertain. That result could be a false positive.
  • can't / couldn't = I'm sure it's NOT true. That can't be right — let me check.
