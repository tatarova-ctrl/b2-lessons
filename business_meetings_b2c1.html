📖Reading text

"The Meeting Has Changed — Have You?"

★ Marcus Larsson · Workplace consultant & corporate trainer

If someone had told me, ten years ago, that a typical Tuesday morning would consist of jumping between four video calls, half of which include a colleague frozen mid-sentence and another colleague forgetting they're on mute, I would have laughed. Yet here we are. Business meetings, as a genre, have changed beyond recognition — and so has the language we use to talk about them.

Today's working professional doesn't just attend a meeting — they jump on a call, hop on Zoom, join a Teams, or, in a particularly cursed phrase that's quietly entered the dictionary, "hop on a quick sync." The verb has become almost sportier. We don't sit down. We jump in. We circle back. We park ideas. Some days, frankly, it feels like an Olympic sport.

"Sorry — can everyone hear me? You're a bit choppy on my end." — Probably said in 84 million meetings yesterday alone.

The pandemic accelerated everything. Hybrid meetings — where some people are in the room and others are dialling in remotely — used to be unusual; now they're the norm. And anyone who's chaired a hybrid meeting knows the unique challenge: keeping everyone engaged when half the participants are technically present but mentally elsewhere, multitasking, or — let's be honest — "on camera, off brain."

So what makes a good business meeting actually work? After fifteen years of consulting, I'd boil it down to three things: a clear agenda, strong facilitation, and genuine outcomes. The rest is noise.

The first — a clear agenda — sounds obvious, but it's the most ignored rule in the corporate world. Far too many meetings kick off with no clear purpose. People show up, glance at the calendar invite, and silently wonder why on earth they're there. A good meeting starts with one simple question: "What does success look like by the end of this hour?" If you can't answer that, the meeting probably shouldn't exist. As the saying goes, "this could've been an email."

The second — facilitation — is an art. A great facilitator reads the room, brings quiet voices in, gently steers the conversation back on track when it drifts, and knows when to park a topic for later. They don't dominate. They orchestrate. Most managers, in my experience, could use some training here. Running a meeting is not the same as attending one.

The third — outcomes — is where most meetings collapse. People throw ideas around for an hour and then leave the room without anyone clearly owning action items or next steps. Without a clear list of "who is doing what by when", even the most lively discussion is, quite frankly, a waste of everyone's time. Wrap up every meeting with a quick recap. It takes thirty seconds and saves hours of confusion later.

Presentations, of course, are a slightly different animal. The big shift here has been towards what I call "data storytelling." Senior leaders today don't want to be buried in slides. They want a clear narrative. They want to know: what's the problem, what's your proposed solution, what's the evidence, and what are you asking me to decide? That's it. If your pitch deck has 47 slides, you've already lost the room.

Modern audiences also have what I'd politely call "Goldfish Attention." If you don't grab their attention in the first 30 seconds, you've lost them. Front-load your key message. Tell them where you're going. Then walk them through the supporting points. End with a clear call to action. Simple, but rarely done well.

And then there's the dreaded Q&A. This is where presentations really live or die. The best presenters I've coached are masters of bridging — they take a tough question and steer it back to one of their core messages. They're never defensive. They welcome the challenge, give a thoughtful response, and move on. If you don't know the answer to a question, just say so. There's no bigger trust-killer than making things up on the spot.

The truth is, business communication isn't getting easier. We've added Slack, video, async tools, AI note-takers and more. But the fundamentals haven't changed. Be clear. Be brief. Be human. If you can do those three things — in person, online or hybrid — you'll stand out from the crowd. Because, frankly, most of your competition can't.

Oh, and one more thing. Please. For the love of God — check that you're off mute before you start talking.

📚Signposting phrasebook

These are the exact phrases native English speakers use to structure a meeting or presentation. Memorise a few from each category — they instantly make you sound more professional.

Opening a meeting
  • Right, let's get started.
  • Thanks everyone for joining.
  • Can everyone hear me OK?
  • Let's kick off with the agenda.
  • Today I'd like to walk you through…
Setting the agenda
  • We've got three things to cover today.
  • The plan for today is as follows…
  • By the end of this call, I'd like us to have decided on…
  • Let's aim to wrap up by half past.
Inviting input
  • What are your thoughts on this?
  • I'd love to hear from Anna on this one.
  • Does anyone want to jump in?
  • Let's bring James in here.
Interrupting politely
  • Sorry to jump in here, but…
  • Can I add something quickly?
  • If I could just jump in for a moment…
  • I'd like to build on what Tom just said.
Disagreeing diplomatically
  • I see your point, but…
  • I'm not sure I fully agree.
  • That's an interesting angle, however…
  • Let me play devil's advocate for a second.
Steering the conversation
  • Let's bring it back to the main point.
  • I think we're going off on a tangent.
  • Let's park that for now.
  • Can we take that one offline?
Presenting data / slides
  • If you look at this slide, you'll see…
  • The data clearly shows that…
  • Let me draw your attention to this number.
  • What this tells us is…
Handling tough questions
  • That's a great question, thanks for raising it.
  • Honestly? I don't have that data to hand, but I'll get back to you.
  • Let me come back to that in a moment.
  • That's something we're actively looking into.
Closing & recap
  • So, to wrap up…
  • Let me quickly recap our action items.
  • I'll send a follow-up email by EOD.
  • Thanks everyone — really productive call.

🎭Role-plays

Practise in pairs. Use signposting language and target expressions. Stay in role for 4–5 minutes, then swap!

◆ ROLE-PLAY 01 · THE WEEKLY SYNC

Running a weekly team meeting

🎬 You're chairing a weekly team meeting on Zoom. There are 5 agenda items but only 30 minutes. One colleague keeps going off on tangents. Another keeps quiet. Run a productive, on-time meeting.
🅰 Meeting chair
  • Open with a clear agenda
  • Bring quiet voices in
  • Politely steer back when someone drifts
  • Park topics that aren't urgent
  • Wrap up with action items
🅱 Team members
  • One of you tends to dominate / go on tangents
  • One of you is quiet but has good ideas
  • One of you is multitasking and not really listening
  • Engage realistically — don't be perfect!
◆ ROLE-PLAY 02 · THE PITCH

Presenting a new idea to senior leadership

🎬 You're pitching a new product idea to a panel of executives. You have 5 minutes to present, then 5 minutes of Q&A. The execs are tired, sceptical, and have very little time.
🅰 The presenter
  • Grab their attention in 30 seconds
  • Walk through the problem and solution
  • Give clear data and next steps
  • End with a strong call to action
  • Field tough questions calmly
🅱 The executives
  • You're tired and a bit sceptical
  • Push back on costs, timeline, risk
  • Ask one tough "what if it fails?" question
  • Be polite but demanding
  • One of you is genuinely interested
◆ ROLE-PLAY 03 · THE DIFFICULT MEETING

Delivering bad news to your team

🎬 You have to tell your team that the project they've been working on for six months has been cancelled. Run a calm, honest, respectful meeting. Don't sugar-coat it, but don't crush morale either.
🅰 The manager
  • Open with honesty, not corporate speak
  • Explain the reasons clearly
  • Acknowledge their hard work
  • Make space for questions and emotions
  • Lay out what comes next
🅱 The team
  • You're shocked, hurt, or angry
  • Ask difficult questions: "Why us?" "What happens to me now?"
  • Don't accept vague answers
  • One of you is more emotional, one is more practical
◆ ROLE-PLAY 04 · HANDLING A TOUGH Q&A

The investor demo gone sideways

🎬 You've just finished pitching to a potential investor. The Q&A starts well, but then they ask three difficult questions in a row — about your competitor, your team's experience, and your unrealistic forecasts.
🅰 The founder
  • Stay calm and welcoming
  • Acknowledge each concern fairly
  • Bridge back to your strengths
  • Don't make things up
  • Offer to follow up where needed
🅱 The investor
  • You're sharp and challenging, not unfriendly
  • Q1: "Your competitor just raised $50M. Why should I back you?"
  • Q2: "Your team has no industry experience."
  • Q3: "These numbers seem wildly optimistic."
  • Watch how they handle it — that's what matters

📝Grammar: Reported Speech & Linking Words

Two grammar areas you genuinely need every day in business communication: reporting what was said in meetings and connecting your ideas clearly when presenting.

▰ Part 1 — Reported speech: the basics

  • Present simple → Past simple: "We're behind schedule" → He said they were behind schedule.
  • Present continuous → Past continuous: "We're testing the new tool" → She said they were testing the new tool.
  • Past simple → Past perfect: "We launched it on Monday" → He said they had launched it on Monday.
  • will → would: "I'll send the deck" → She said she would send the deck.
  • Questions: remove do/does/did, statement word order. "What did you decide?" → He asked what we had decided.
  • Commands: told / asked + object + to + infinitive. "Send the recap by Friday" → She told me to send the recap by Friday.

▰ Part 2 — Linking words for presentations

  • Adding: moreover, furthermore, in addition, on top of that
  • Contrasting: however, on the other hand, although, despite, whereas
  • Cause / result: therefore, as a result, consequently, due to, because of
  • Examples: for instance, such as, namely, in particular
  • Conclusion: in summary, to sum up, all in all, to wrap up
Use these to structure ideas. They make presentations sound polished and professional.
