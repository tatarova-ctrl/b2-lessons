📘 B1 · CLEAR VERSION

Data Centres Are Everywhere

Every time you watch a video, send a message or ask an AI a question, you are using a data centre. A data centre is a huge building full of computers called servers. These servers store information and run the apps we use every day.

In the last few years, data centres have appeared all over the world. People say they are popping up like mushrooms after rain. The reason is simple: streaming, online shopping and especially AI all need a lot of computer power.

A data centre is not just a room full of computers. It needs strong cooling systems, because the machines get very hot. It also needs backup power in case the electricity goes off.

The biggest data centres belong to Amazon, Google and Microsoft. These three companies are called hyperscalers because their centres are so big. Other companies, like Equinix, rent space to smaller businesses.

But there is a problem. Data centres use a huge amount of electricity. One large data centre can use as much energy as a small city. They also need a lot of water. Some people say the industry is not doing enough to protect the environment.

Governments want the jobs and money that data centres bring. But they also worry about the energy and water. In Dublin, Ireland, the government had to stop building new data centres for a while. The local electricity system could not cope.

Data centres are the new railways of our century. The question is not whether to build them, but how to build them in a smart and fair way.

📕 C1 · ADVANCED VERSION

Popping Up Like Mushrooms After Rain

There's a phrase I keep hearing from journalists and planners: data centres are popping up like mushrooms after rain. Honestly, it's no exaggeration. Planning applications for hyperscale facilities have hit record levels across Europe, the US and Southeast Asia. The AI boom, the explosion in cloud computing, and the relentless growth of streaming and digital workloads have created demand the industry is struggling to keep pace with.

A data centre isn't just a big room full of computers. It's an enormously complex piece of critical infrastructure — as essential as a power station. Inside, you'll find thousands of server racks, intricate cooling systems, and redundant power supplies. The best facilities have a Power Usage Effectiveness (PUE) close to 1.0 — meaning almost no wasted energy.

"Every time someone says 'the cloud', what they really mean is someone else's computer in a warehouse the size of an aircraft hangar, somewhere outside Dublin or Frankfurt."

The problem comes with a staggering environmental cost. A single large facility can consume as much electricity as a small city. Campaigners argue the industry is greenwashing — making loud claims about renewables while drawing on fossil-fuel-heavy grids. The debate is far from settled.

Governments are caught between two forces: the jobs and prestige of hosting digital infrastructure, and legitimate pressure from communities about land, energy and water. Ireland briefly imposed a moratorium on new connections in Dublin because the grid couldn't cope with the load.

It's not just a technology story. It's a story about data sovereignty — which countries control where the world's information sits. If I had to cut through the noise, I'd say data centres are the railways of the 21st century. The real question isn't whether to build them, but whether we're building them smartly and sustainably.

📝Grammar focus

Two grammar areas linked to discussions about data centres. B1 items are foundations; C1 items go further.

GRAMMAR 1🔄 Conditionals & reported speech

📘 B1 essentials → 📕 C1 stretch

  • Zero conditional (facts): If + present, present → If servers get hot, they fail.
  • First conditional (real future): If + present, will → If we invest now, we will reduce emissions.
  • Second conditional (hypothetical now): If + past, would → If everyone cared, we'd act faster.
  • Third conditional (unreal past): If + past perfect, would have + p.p. → If Ireland had built more capacity, it wouldn't have imposed a moratorium.
  • Mixed conditional (C1): past condition → present result. If they had invested earlier, we wouldn't be in this position now.
  • Reported speech: tenses shift back: "We are expanding." → He said they were expanding. Reported questions use statement word order: "Where is the centre?" → She asked where the centre was.
UNREAL PAST → If + had + p.p., would have + p.p.
REPORTED → tenses move back one step; no do/did; if/whether for yes-no

GRAMMAR 2🔧 Modal verbs · obligation, ability & deduction

📘 B1 → 📕 C1 across one grammar point

  • Obligation: must (personal/strong), have to (external rule). Operators have to follow EU rules.
  • Prohibition: mustn't / can't. You can't build here.
  • No obligation: don't have to, needn't. You don't have to be an engineer to understand it.
  • Ability now: can · past ability: could (general) / was able to (specific success).
  • Present deduction: must (sure true) / can't (sure false) / might/could (possible).
  • Past deduction (C1): must have / can't have / might have + p.p. The grid must have failed.
OBLIGATION → must / have to · PROHIBITION → mustn't / can't
PAST DEDUCTION → modal + have + p.p.

🎤Speaking practice

Eight tasks. The first four are friendlier B1 prompts; the last four push you into C1 discussion territory. Try to use at least 3 words or phrases from the lesson in each answer.

💬 Useful phrases

Opening: "From what I understand…" / "If I had to sum it up…"
Opinion: "I tend to think…" / "I'd argue that…"
Conceding then pushing back: "That's a fair point, but…" / "I see that, however…"
Speculating: "It might be that…" / "It must have been…"
Conditionals in use: "If governments invested more, …" / "If they had acted earlier, …"
