💰Money at Work & Salary Talk

The conversation about money at work that nobody really teaches you. Highlighted items are phrasal verbs and collocations — learn them.

The Conversation Nobody Teaches You

★ Sophia Kennedy · Personal finance coach · Helps young professionals talk about money

Most of us learn about money the hard way. School teaches us maths, but it rarely teaches us how to set up a budget, ask for a raise, or put money aside when we already feel broke. After ten years of coaching young professionals, I can tell you the truth: people don't fail at money because they're bad with numbers. They fail because nobody ever sat them down and explained the basics.

Let's start with the most useful word: take-home pay. Your salary is one number; your take-home pay — what actually lands in your bank account after tax — is another. Always plan with the second one. Many people spend beyond their means simply because they get carried away by the bigger number on the contract.

“You don't need to earn more to feel less stressed about money. You need to know exactly where it goes.”

The next step is to keep track of your spending. Use an app, a notebook, anything. Most people are quietly shocked when they add up their monthly small treats. The morning coffee, the streaming services, the takeaway lunches — alone they're nothing; together they often add up to several hundred euros a month. None of this is bad, by the way. But you should choose it, not drift into it.

Then there's the famous habit: put money aside as soon as you get paid. Don't wait to see what's left. The professionals I admire most automatically stash away 10–20% of their salary the day it arrives. They save up for a rainy day and they build up an emergency fund of three months' expenses. Boring? Yes. Life-changing? Also yes.

Pay off your debt faster than you think you should. Credit card interest is the most expensive form of money there is. If you can only manage a small extra payment each month, do it. Watching debt go down is one of the most motivating things in adult life.

And then there's the awkward part: asking for more money. When you ask for a raise, don't talk about your bills. Talk about your value. Show what you've done over the last year, what you'll do next year, and the salary range for your role. Then stop talking. Silence in salary talks is your friend. Most people chicken out at this point. Don't.

Finally, splash out sometimes. Saving every euro makes you miserable. Pick the few things you genuinely love, spend on them happily, and cut back on everything else. Money is supposed to serve your life. Most people quietly serve their money instead. So get on top of your finances early, talk about them honestly, and you'll never need to make ends meet in fear again.

📧Business Communication & Emails

The polite phrases, phrasal verbs and collocations that quietly run every office. Targets are in gold.

The 30 Seconds That Shape Your Day

★ Daniel Park · Communication trainer · Helps teams write less and say more

Modern office life happens through email. We fire off emails between meetings, we get back to people late, we follow up on people who never replied, we cc too many colleagues just in case. After fifteen years training teams, I can tell you the surprising truth: 80% of the way you sound at work is decided in the first 30 seconds of every email. Get that right, and the rest of your day gets easier.

Start with a clear, kind opening. “I hope you're well” is fine. “Quick question for you” is better. “I'm following up on our chat last Wednesday” is the best of all, because it reminds the reader exactly who you are and why you're writing. Vague openings make busy people put off answering.

“Your first sentence either makes a busy reader read on, or quietly invites them to deal with you tomorrow.”

Then get to the point. The biggest mistake B2 learners make is to warm up for three paragraphs before the actual request. Senior people don't have time. State what you want in the first three lines. Use bullet points if you have several questions. Then the polite closing.

When you need to chase someone, do it kindly. “Just touching base in case my last message got lost in your inbox” is gentle and effective. “I sent over the file last week — could you get back to me by Friday?” is firm but polite. Avoid “As per my last email” — it sounds tired and faintly rude.

Sometimes you need to loop people in. “I'm looping in Anna, who handles invoices,” is clear and useful. “I'm cc'ing in my manager so we're all on the same page” signals that the matter is now official. Use it carefully; cc'ing your boss into every email looks insecure.

Bad news needs care. Don't beat around the bush, but cushion the blow. “Unfortunately, we won't be able to go ahead this quarter, but I'd love to pick this up again in March” is professional and human. “Sorry, no” sounds cold, even when it's accurate.

Finally, two phrases worth memorising. “Let me run something by you” softens any request for advice. “Let's take this offline” ends a long email chain without offending anyone. They both buy you time, protect relationships and quietly make you sound like a senior. Keep emails short, kind and specific. Your colleagues will get back to you faster, and they'll quietly think of you as a pleasure to work with.

🤝Customer Service & Complaints

The collocations and phrasal verbs you really need to deal with angry customers and turn things round. Targets are in gold.

The Angry Email at 7 a.m.

★ Maya Reilly · Customer service trainer · Has handled 15,000 complaints

Every customer service career starts with an angry email at 7 a.m. After fifteen thousand complaints, I'll tell you a simple truth: customers don't really get upset because something went wrong. They get upset because they feel ignored when it did. The first job of a great customer service person isn't to fix the product. It's to make the customer feel heard.

Step one: read between the lines. Most angry emails are not really about the broken order. They're about a bad week, a tight budget, a feeling of being treated like a number. When you reply, acknowledge the human first. “I completely understand how frustrating this must be” goes a long way before you even mention a solution.

Step two: look into the problem properly. Don't promise anything until you know the facts. “Let me look into this and get back to you within two hours” is a perfect sentence. It buys you time, shows respect, and stops you making promises you might have to back out of.

“A customer remembers two things about a complaint: how angry they were at the start, and how cared-for they felt at the end. Make the second one bigger than the first.”

Step three: own up to the mistake. Don't blame the courier, the supplier, the weather, or your colleague. The customer doesn't care. “We got this wrong, and I'm sorry” is one of the most powerful sentences in business. Most companies shy away from it, which is why the ones that say it stand out so much.

Step four: make up for the mistake in a way the customer didn't expect. A small refund. A free replacement. A handwritten note. Don't fob them off with a 5% discount; that often backfires. Real generosity is remembered for years.

Now, what about really difficult customers? Some people will fly off the handle no matter what you do. Stay calm. Don't lose your temper. Don't take it personally — they're not really angry at you, even when it sounds that way. Lower your voice, slow down your speech, and ask one calm question: “What would make this right for you today?” That single sentence often defuses the worst conversations.

And then there's the awkward truth: not every customer is right. A small number behave so badly that they damage your team. Let them go. “We don't seem to be the right fit for you. We'll refund your last order in full,” is sometimes the only sensible answer. Protecting your team's morale is part of customer service too.

If you take one idea, take this. Set things right faster than the customer expects, follow through on what you promised, and write the kind of message that you'd want to receive on a bad morning. Customer service done well isn't a cost. It's the cheapest, kindest marketing a company can do.

🌱Sustainability & Green Business

How real companies cut down on waste, switch over to cleaner systems, and take responsibility. Targets are in gold.

The Quiet Cost of Cheap

★ Helena Jørgensen · Sustainability consultant · Advises medium-sized businesses

Twenty years ago, “green business” meant a small recycling box in the office and a poster about saving paper. Today it is one of the biggest forces reshaping every industry. Customers ask harder questions. Employees want to work for companies they're proud of. Governments slowly bring in new rules. And the cheap, wasteful way of doing things is quietly becoming the expensive one.

Most companies start in the same place. They cut down on paper. They switch off the lights at night. They phase out single-use plastics in the canteen. These are easy wins, but they are not the whole story. Real sustainability isn't a poster on the wall; it's a change in how the business actually works.

“Cheap is not really cheap. Somebody else — a worker, a river, a forest — is quietly paying the price.”

The next step is energy. Many companies now switch over to green electricity, install solar panels, or insulate buildings properly. Heating an office costs less when you don't throw away half the heat through the windows. Some businesses run on 100% renewable energy. It used to be expensive; now it's often cheaper.

Then there's the question of what a company makes. Smart businesses are starting to look after the whole life cycle of their product. They get rid of packaging that nobody needs. They design things that last and can be repaired. They take back old products for recycling. It's slower, but customers cotton on quickly to the difference.

Supply chains are the hardest part. Most pollution doesn't happen inside the office. It happens in factories, fields and mines far away. Real green companies look into who their suppliers are, what they pay their workers, and how their materials are produced. Greenwashing — pretending to be more sustainable than you are — usually falls apart the moment a journalist starts asking questions.

There's also the social side. Taking responsibility for the planet often goes hand in hand with treating people better. Better salaries, safer factories, fair contracts. The companies winning the next decade aren't only “green” in the colour-of-the-logo sense. They're trying to do less harm at every step.

Of course, this is hard. Small businesses can't splash out on expensive new equipment. Customers say they care, then choose the cheapest option anyway. Politicians promise change, then back down when it gets unpopular. Burnout among sustainability staff is a real and quiet problem.

But the direction is clear. Every business will have to take action on its carbon footprint. Every employee will need to understand a little about energy, waste, and supply chains. “Going green” isn't a side project anymore; it's becoming part of normal management. So get on top of the basics now, ask honest questions, and remember: small daily choices, repeated by millions of people, really do make a difference.
