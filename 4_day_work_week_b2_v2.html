📖Reading

Read about real people and real companies testing the 4-day work week — and what actually happened.

Four Days. Same Pay. Here's What Happened.

Future of Work | The Guardian Weekly | 2025

Charlotte Moore is a graphic designer at a marketing agency in Bristol. Every Friday, while her friends commute to offices, Charlotte goes hiking, visits her mother, or simply catches up on sleep. She hasn't taken a sick day in eight months. Her productivity scores are the highest in her team. Her company switched to the four-day week two years ago — and has never looked back.

"I used to drag myself through Friday afternoons achieving absolutely nothing," she says. "Now I do the same work in four days because I actually want to be there." Charlotte's experience is not unusual. In a landmark 2022 UK trial involving 61 companies and nearly 3,000 employees, the results spoke for themselves: revenue stayed the same, sick days fell by 65%, and 92% of companies chose to keep the model permanently. The trial operated on what researchers called the 100-80-100 principle — 100% of the salary, 80% of the hours, 100% of the output. The idea sounds radical until you examine how most people actually spend their working hours.

Microsoft Japan ran its own experiment in 2019. It closed offices on Fridays for one month and cut meeting times to 30 minutes. Productivity jumped 40%. Emails were replaced by instant messages. People stopped padding out their day with performative busyness and focused on what actually mattered. Meanwhile in Iceland, a national four-day week trial ran from 2015 to 2019 with 2,500 workers — over 1% of the entire working population. Researchers found workers were less stressed, more energised, and just as productive. By 2021, 86% of Iceland's workforce had moved to shorter hours or gained the right to request them.

Critics argue the model doesn't translate to every sector. A nurse, a firefighter, or a restaurant chef cannot simply compress their schedule. These are fair points. But supporters counter that the four-day week isn't a rigid law — it's a shift in thinking: the idea that hours worked is a poor measure of value created. If Charlotte can produce more in four days than she used to in five, the question isn't whether the model works. The question is why we took so long to try it.

📚Vocabulary

Study these key terms about work, productivity, and workplace change.

productivity
The efficiency of work — how much output is produced per unit of time or effort.
Productivity rose 40% when Microsoft Japan switched to a 4-day week.
burnout
Extreme exhaustion caused by prolonged stress, overwork, or lack of rest.
Burnout costs European businesses an estimated €620 billion per year.
wellbeing
The state of being healthy, comfortable, and happy — beyond just physical health.
Employee wellbeing improved dramatically in all four-day week trials.
pilot programme
A small-scale trial to test whether a new idea works before rolling it out fully.
The UK pilot programme involved 61 companies over six months.
work-life balance
The equilibrium between time spent working and time for personal life.
Four-day week advocates say work-life balance improves dramatically.
absenteeism
The habit of regularly being absent from work, often due to stress or illness.
Absenteeism fell by 65% in companies on the four-day model.
output
The amount of work, goods, or results produced in a given time.
The 100-80-100 principle demands 100% output for 80% of the hours.
legislation
Laws or a set of laws made by government; the process of making laws.
Several countries are considering legislation to make the 4-day week standard.
flexible working
An arrangement allowing employees control over when, where, or how much they work.
Flexible working has become a top priority for job seekers post-COVID.
performative busyness
The act of appearing busy at work without actually achieving meaningful results.
The four-day week exposes how much time is lost to performative busyness.

🎯Common Collocations

Learn these natural word combinations related to work, schedules, and productivity.

💼 Work and time

  • working hours = the hours during which a person works. The debate centres on reducing working hours without cutting pay.
  • sick days = days taken off work due to illness. Sick days fell by 65% in companies trialling the four-day week.
  • annual leave = paid holiday time a worker is entitled to each year. Some employers confuse the four-day week with reducing annual leave — they are different.
  • cut meeting times = to reduce the length or number of meetings. Microsoft Japan cut meeting times to 30 minutes and saw productivity soar.

📈 Performance and results

  • boost productivity = to increase how efficiently work gets done. Shorter weeks can boost productivity by eliminating wasted time.
  • revenue stays the same = income does not decrease. In 92% of trials, revenue stayed the same or increased.
  • employee retention = the ability to keep workers from leaving. The four-day week dramatically improves employee retention.
  • measure of value = the standard used to assess how much something is worth. Hours worked is a poor measure of value created.

🔄 Change and transition

  • shift in thinking = a fundamental change in attitude or approach. The four-day week represents a shift in thinking about work.
  • roll out = to introduce a new system or product gradually at scale. Several governments plan to roll out four-day week legislation by 2026.
  • compress the schedule = to fit the same work into fewer days. Not every sector can simply compress the schedule without problems.
  • gain the right to request = to become legally entitled to ask for something. 86% of Iceland's workforce gained the right to request shorter hours.

➡️ Conditional Types — Quick Reminder

  • 1st Conditional (real future): If + present simple → will/won't + verb. If companies adopt this, productivity will improve.
  • 2nd Conditional (hypothetical present): If + past simple → would + verb. If I had Fridays off, I would finally learn to cook.
  • 3rd Conditional (impossible past): If + had + past participle → would have + past participle. If they had tried this sooner, workers would have been healthier.

🔄 Phrasal Verbs from the Reading — Meanings

  • catch up on = to do something you didn't have time for before. Charlotte uses Fridays to catch up on sleep and personal errands.
  • look back = to think about or return to a previous situation. 92% of companies never looked back after adopting the model.
  • drag yourself through = to force yourself to do something difficult with no energy. She used to drag herself through pointless Friday meetings.
  • pad out = to make something longer or fuller than it needs to be. Workers stop padding out their days with unnecessary tasks.
  • roll out = to introduce something gradually at a wider scale. Governments plan to roll out four-day week legislation by 2026.
  • bring about = to cause something to happen. New legislation could bring about a major shift in working culture.
  • stick with = to continue using or doing something; to not abandon it. 92% of companies chose to stick with the model permanently.
