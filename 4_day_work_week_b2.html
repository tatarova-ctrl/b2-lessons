The Four-Day Work Week: From Pipe Dream to Global Reality

By James Mitchell | Business & Society Quarterly | 2025

For decades, the five-day work week has been considered the gold standard in employment. However, this assumption is rapidly coming under scrutiny as a transformative global movement challenges our most fundamental beliefs about work. The four-day work week—a concept that once seemed impossible—is now gaining momentum at an unprecedented pace. What was dismissed as a pipe dream just a few years ago is proving to be achievable, with compelling evidence emerging from trials around the world.

Between 2022 and 2025, more than 8,700 employees across multiple countries participated in coordinated trials of the four-day work week model, primarily using the 100-80-100 principle: 100% pay for 80% of the time while maintaining 100% productivity. The results have been nothing short of remarkable. Companies participating in these trials reported that employees experienced significantly reduced stress and burnout, while simultaneously reporting better physical and mental health outcomes. Workers gained an extra day to recharge their batteries, pursue hobbies, spend time with family, or tackle household responsibilities. Perhaps most importantly for employers, business profits remained stable or even increased, while employee turnover virtually disappeared.

Major corporations have jumped on the bandwagon, recognizing the transformative power of this model. Microsoft Japan documented a 40% productivity gain when they closed offices on Fridays and reduced meeting times. Buffer, a fully remote company, found that after switching to a four-day week, productivity increased by 22%, job applications surged 88%, and absenteeism plummeted 66%. These companies are not isolated cases—around 92% of participating organizations chose to stick with the new arrangement after their trials, citing exceptional results and employee satisfaction.

Despite these successes, skepticism remains. Critics argue that the four-day work week could be a double-edged sword for certain industries. Service sectors, customer-facing businesses, and healthcare cannot simply scale back operations without affecting customer availability. Additionally, compressing five days of work into four could create psychological stress for some workers, as they struggle to keep up with the pace. Some worry that the model will only benefit privileged white-collar workers, potentially widening inequality for blue-collar laborers who cannot adapt their work in the same way.

Looking forward, governments are beginning to take notice. Iceland, Germany, and the United Kingdom have all conducted or are conducting national trials, suggesting that policy change is possible. Trade unions in Mexico and Ireland are actively campaigning for legislation that would bring about this change at a national level. If this momentum continues, the four-day work week could genuinely become the new normal, marking a revolutionary shift in how we view work, productivity, and human wellbeing. The question is no longer whether the four-day week works—the evidence is overwhelming. The question is now whether employers and governments have the courage to embrace this transformation and fundamentally reshape the future of work.

🎯Common Collocations

Learn common word combinations used when discussing work and productivity.

💼 Collocations with work and employment

  • work-life balance = equilibrium between professional and personal time. The four-day week improves work-life balance dramatically.
  • employee satisfaction = contentment and happiness of workers. Companies report high employee satisfaction after implementing the change.
  • stress levels = amount of pressure or tension experienced. Stress levels decreased among workers with an extra day off.
  • workplace culture = the shared values and behaviors within an organization. A four-day week transforms workplace culture positively.

📊 Collocations with business results

  • boost productivity = to increase efficiency and output. The change boosted productivity by surprising amounts.
  • cut costs = to reduce expenses or spending. Companies can cut costs through reduced energy usage with the four-day model.
  • drive growth = to promote expansion and improvement. Better employee health drives business growth long-term.
  • maintain profitability = to keep profits stable despite changes. Companies maintained profitability despite working fewer hours.

🔄 Collocations with change and progress

  • gain momentum = to increase in speed, strength, or popularity. The four-day week movement is gaining momentum globally.
  • come under scrutiny = to be examined critically or investigated closely. Traditional work models are coming under scrutiny.
  • jump on the bandwagon = to join others in doing something popular. Many companies jumped on the bandwagon after seeing successful trials.
  • embrace change = to accept and adopt something willingly. Progressive employers embrace change readily.
