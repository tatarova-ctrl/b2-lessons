"The Race Is On — and Europe's Carmakers Are Sweating"

★ Industry analysis · April 2026

For more than a hundred years, the global car industry was effectively dominated by a small group of legacy carmakers — names like Volkswagen, Toyota, Ford and General Motors. They built cars, sold them around the world, and had relatively little to worry about beyond fuel prices and economic cycles. That comfortable era is well and truly over. Today, the industry is facing what many analysts describe as a perfect storm: aggressive Chinese competition, tightening regulation, the EV transition, and — for European brands in particular — a serious case of "are we already too late?"

Let's start with the obvious. China has overtaken Germany and Japan as the world's largest car exporter. BYD, the Chinese giant, now sells more electric vehicles globally than Tesla. Brands that most Europeans had never heard of five years ago — NIO, XPeng, Zeekr, Geely's premium lines — are now flooding European markets with stylish, well-built EVs at prices that legacy brands simply can't match. It's hard to overstate just how dramatic the shift has been.

The European response has been mixed, to say the least. In 2024, the EU imposed punitive tariffs of up to 35% on Chinese EV imports, arguing that Beijing was unfairly subsidising its own manufacturers. Critics called it protectionism in all but name. Supporters countered that without a level playing field, European factories — and the millions of jobs that depend on them — simply wouldn't survive.

"The European auto industry isn't just losing market share — it's losing time. And in this race, time is everything." — automotive consultant, Munich

At the same time, regulation is tightening across the board. The EU has set increasingly ambitious emissions targets: from 2035, no new petrol or diesel cars can be sold in the bloc. Every legacy carmaker has had to rip up their long-term plans and pour billions into battery technology, charging infrastructure and software development — areas where, frankly, most of them were caught napping. Volkswagen recently announced its biggest restructuring in decades, including factory closures in Germany. Just a few years ago, the idea of VW shutting plants on home soil would have been unthinkable.

The United States, meanwhile, is playing a different game entirely. Through the Inflation Reduction Act, Washington has poured tens of billions of dollars into reshoring battery manufacturing, attracting investment, and blocking Chinese competitors through both tariffs and supply-chain rules. President Biden's tariffs on Chinese EVs were jaw-dropping — up to 100%. The new Trump administration has gone even further. The result? Chinese carmakers have effectively been locked out of the US market, but European brands, ironically, have found themselves caught in the crossfire.

So where does this all leave the industry? In short, at a crossroads. The next decade will be defined by who can produce affordable EVs at scale, who controls the battery supply chain, and who manages to navigate an increasingly fragmented global trade system. Some legacy brands will weather the storm. Others, almost certainly, will not. Mergers, partnerships and even outright failures are back on the table for the first time since the 2008 financial crisis.

For European policymakers, the dilemma is uncomfortable. Open the doors to cheap Chinese EVs and you accelerate the green transition — but you risk hollowing out your own industry. Slam the doors shut, and consumers pay more, the transition slows, and you trigger a trade war you may not win. There are no easy answers, and frankly, the clock is ticking.

One thing is certain: the comfortable century-long dominance of Western carmakers is over. The race for the future of mobility has well and truly begun — and the leaders of 2035 are unlikely to be the same as those of 2015.

📝Grammar: Cause & Effect + Conditionals

Two grammar areas you genuinely need to discuss industry trends and policy: cause & effect connectors (because of, due to, leading to, as a result…) and conditionals for predictions and hypotheticals.

🚗 Part 1 — Cause & effect language

  • because / since / as + clause. BYD is winning because it produces at huge scale.
  • because of / due to / owing to + noun. Sales fell due to high interest rates.
  • as a result of / thanks to + noun. As a result of tariffs, prices have risen.
  • so / therefore / consequently / as a result = "and the result is". The market collapsed; consequently, factories closed.
  • This led to / This has resulted in + noun / -ing. The 2035 ban has led to massive investment in EVs.
+ NOUN: due to / because of / owing to / as a result of / thanks to
+ CLAUSE: because / since / as
CONNECTING TWO IDEAS: so / therefore / consequently / as a result / this led to

🚗 Part 2 — Conditionals for industry analysis

  • 1st conditional — real future. If tariffs stay in place, prices will rise.
  • 2nd conditional — hypothetical present. If I were a European CEO, I would invest in batteries today.
  • 3rd conditional — past hypothetical / regret. If carmakers had acted earlier, they wouldn't have lost so much ground.
  • Mixed — past condition → present result. If they had invested in EVs 10 years ago, they would still be profitable now.
