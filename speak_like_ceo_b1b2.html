"It's Not What CEOs Say — It's How They Say It"

★ Maria Bennett · Executive coach · Leadership column

I've spent fifteen years coaching senior leaders, and there's one thing I tell every single client: people don't follow your title — they follow your tone. The most successful CEOs I've ever worked with weren't always the smartest in the room, the loudest, or the most experienced. But they all had one thing in common — they spoke with purpose. Every word counted. And here's the good news: this is a skill you can learn, no matter where you are in your career.

The first thing CEOs do differently is that they get to the point. They don't ramble. They don't open with three minutes of small talk before mentioning what the meeting is about. They lead with the headline. "Here's what we need to decide. Here's why it matters. Here's what I recommend." That's it. No fluff. No filler.

The second thing is that they own the room — without dominating it. They speak less than you might think, but when they do speak, they say something worth listening to. They ask sharp questions. They steer the conversation when it drifts. And critically, they make people feel heard, even when they ultimately disagree. As one of my mentors once told me:

"Leadership isn't about having the loudest voice in the room. It's about being the person whose voice the room actually wants to hear."

The third habit — and this one's harder than it sounds — is that great CEOs take ownership. When something goes wrong, they don't blame the team, the market, or the previous CEO. They simply say, "We made a mistake. Here's what we're going to do about it." That kind of honesty is rare, and it's incredibly powerful. It builds trust faster than any other behaviour I know.

Now, here's where it gets practical. Most professionals — even very talented ones — accidentally undermine themselves by using weak language. Phrases like "I just wanted to ask…", "I'm not sure if this makes sense, but…", or "sorry to bother you" are so common they sound automatic. But they quietly tell people: "don't take me seriously." Strong leaders are direct without being aggressive. They say, "I have a question," not "Sorry, can I just quickly ask…" They say, "My recommendation is…", not "Maybe we could possibly think about…"

Another thing CEOs do brilliantly is frame things positively — without lying. There's a huge difference between "We failed to hit our targets" and "We fell short of our ambitious goals, and here's what we're learning." Same facts, completely different energy. Word choice shapes how people feel. And how people feel determines how they act.

One of my favourite CEO habits is borrowing language from data. They say things like, "The numbers tell us…", "What we're seeing in the market is…", or "If you look at the trend over the past six months…" This kind of phrasing instantly makes them sound thoughtful and grounded — even if their actual gut feeling is doing most of the work.

Finally — and this is huge — great CEOs are comfortable with silence. They don't fill every pause. They don't apologise for thinking. They let questions land. When someone asks them something tough, they pause for a moment, then answer with calm confidence. Silence, used well, is one of the most powerful tools you have.

The truth is, you don't need a corner office or a fancy title to start speaking like a CEO. Pick two or three habits from this list. Try them in your next meeting. Notice how people respond. Confidence isn't a personality trait — it's a practice. And like any practice, it gets stronger every time you use it.

🎤Speaking practice — sound senior in real situations

Speak 1½–2 minutes per question. Use at least 3 target expressions per answer. Channel your inner CEO!

◆ TASK 01 · INTRODUCE YOURSELF LIKE A CEO
Imagine you're walking into a room of senior people. Introduce yourself in 60 seconds — your role, what you bring, what you stand for. No "sorry", no "just".
Try to use: a track record · to deliver results · to bring to the table · to lead by example · I'm convinced that
◆ TASK 02 · DEFEND YOUR OPINION
Pick something you genuinely believe about your work or life. State it as a CEO would — directly, with reasons. No hedging!
Try to use: in my view · my recommendation · make no mistake · the bottom line · I'm convinced that
◆ TASK 03 · OWN A MISTAKE
Tell us about a real mistake you made at work. How would a CEO talk about it — without blame, without excuses?
Try to use: to take ownership · I take ownership of this · to fall short · here's what we're learning · accountability
◆ TASK 04 · GIVE A 60-SECOND UPDATE
Give a short, sharp update on a real project at your work. Lead with the headline. Three points maximum. End with a clear next step.
Try to use: here's where we are · the headline is · my recommendation · let's get to it · stakeholders
◆ TASK 05 · PUSH BACK DIPLOMATICALLY
Your boss has just given you an unrealistic deadline. Push back like a leader — no anger, no apologising. Be calm, clear, and constructive.
Try to use: to push back · I see it differently · let me play devil's advocate · here's what's realistic · keep my cool
◆ TASK 06 · INSPIRE A TEAM
Imagine you're leading a tired team after a tough quarter. Give a short inspiring talk to motivate them again.
Try to use: to raise the bar · to think big · I have full confidence · this team is capable · at the end of the day
◆ TASK 07 · HANDLE A HARD QUESTION
The teacher will throw you 3 tough questions one after another. Buy time, stay calm, answer with confidence.
Sample tough questions: "Why should we promote you?" / "What was your biggest failure?" / "Tell us about a time you disagreed with your boss."
◆ TASK 08 · QUICK-FIRE · 30 SECONDS EACH
No planning. Just talk!
▸ Sum up your career so far in three sentences.
▸ The one thing you'd change about how meetings happen at your company.
▸ Complete: "If I were CEO for a day, the first decision I'd make is…"
▸ One CEO whose communication style you admire — and why.
▸ The single most important quality of a great leader.

📝Grammar: Sounding Confident — Grammar Choices

Confident speech isn't just about words — it's about grammar. Two patterns that instantly upgrade how you sound: active voice instead of weak passive, and strong modal verbs instead of weak hedging.

★ Part 1 — Active vs passive voice

  • Active = strong, confident: I made the decision. / The team delivered the results.
  • Passive = often weaker, hides who did it: The decision was made. / Mistakes were made.
  • Use the active voice when you want to own your actions and decisions. CEOs almost always use active.
Active: I + verb + object  →  sounds confident, accountable

★ Part 2 — Strong vs weak modals

  • Confident: will, need to, have to, am going to, it's clear that
  • Weak / hedging (avoid in confident speech): might, could maybe, kind of, sort of, I'm not sure if…, hopefully
  • Of course, some hedging is polite. But cut excess hedging — that's where leaders sound weak.
Weak: "I think maybe we could possibly try…" → Strong: "We will…" / "My recommendation is…"

Part A — make it active. Rewrite each weak passive sentence in the active voice. Type just the new verb phrase.

01"The decision was made by me." → I the decision.

02"Mistakes were made." → We mistakes.

03"Targets were missed last quarter." → We our targets last quarter.

04"The strategy is being reviewed." → We the strategy.

05"The new plan was created by the leadership team." → The leadership team the new plan.

Part B — make it stronger. Rewrite the weak version using a confident modal/expression.

06"I think maybe we could possibly try this approach." → My recommendation this approach. ("is to" + verb)

07"Hopefully we'll deliver on time." → We on time. ("will" + verb)

08"It might be a good idea to invest more." → We more. ("need to" + verb)

09"I'm not sure if this is right, but maybe…" → I'm this is the right call. (2 words)

10"Sorry, I was just wondering if perhaps…" → I . (3 words)
