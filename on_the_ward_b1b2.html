🛏️At the Bedside

How a good nurse opens a visit — what to ask, what to check, how to build trust in three minutes. Useful phrases are in gold.

The First Three Minutes Decide the Whole Visit

★ Sister Olivia Brennan · Senior nurse · Twenty years in private rehabilitation

In rehabilitation, you visit each patient many times a day. The first minutes of every visit really matter. If you walk in tired and silent, the patient feels it. If you walk in calm and friendly, they relax — and the rest of the visit goes much better. You don't need extra time. You just need a clear routine.

I always start with a smile and the patient's name. “Good morning, Mrs Lopez. How are you feeling today?” Then I check their identity at the start of every shift: “Can you tell me your full name and date of birth?” It feels formal, but it keeps patients safe. People in rehab often share rooms and have similar names. Mistakes are easy.

A patient who trusts you will tell you about a small pain at 7 a.m. A patient who doesn't will hide it until midnight.

Next come the vital signs: blood pressure, pulse, temperature, breathing rate, and oxygen saturation. I always explain before I touch the patient. “I'm just going to check your blood pressure, it won't take a minute.” Patients in private clinics expect this kind of small politeness, and it lowers their anxiety.

Then I check their comfort: pillows, water, the position of the bed. A patient in pain or sitting badly will never enjoy their session of physiotherapy in the afternoon. Five seconds spent fixing a pillow can save you an hour of trouble later.

I also ask one quiet, open question every morning: “Is there anything worrying you?” Many older patients won't complain; they don't want to be a problem. That single question often brings out a real concern — a phone call they're waiting for, a fear about tomorrow's treatment, a small symptom they didn't think mattered.

Before I leave, I always tell them when I'll be back. “I'll be back in ten minutes with your tablets.” Patients in rehab can feel lonely between visits. A simple promise of return makes the waiting easier. Some of my best feedback over the years has come from patients who said, “You always told me when you were coming back. I felt safe.” That sentence, after twenty years, still tells me the same thing: in nursing, the small things are not the small things.

💊The Medication Round

How to give medicines safely — the right patient, the right pill, the right reason — and how to explain each one in clear English. Useful phrases are in gold.

Right Patient, Right Pill, Right Reason

★ Nurse Ahmad Reza · Senior medication nurse · Trains new staff at a private clinic

The medication round is the part of the shift where mistakes hurt the most. In a private rehab clinic, most patients take several pills every day. Some take more than ten. They need someone calm, careful, and never in a hurry. Speed feels efficient. It isn't. A small error in dosage can put a patient back in hospital for a week.

We follow a simple rule called the “five rights”: right patient, right medication, right dose, right time, right route. Every nurse I train repeats this in the corridor every morning. Before any tablet goes into a patient's hand, I ask, “Can you tell me your full name and date of birth?” — even with a patient I've known for two weeks. Then I check the chart, then the box. Two checks, no shortcuts.

Patients forgive almost anything except feeling unsafe with their medication. Take your time. Always.

I explain each pill in simple English. “This little blue one is for your blood pressure. The white one is for your cholesterol. This is a painkiller — take it only if you need it.” Patients in private clinics often ask many questions. That's a good thing. A patient who asks questions is a patient who notices changes.

I always check for allergies, even when it's written in the chart. “Are you allergic to any medications?” Some patients only remember a strong reaction when you ask them directly. I also warn about common side effects. “You may feel a little drowsy. Don't try to get up alone afterwards.” Patients respect honesty far more than fake confidence.

Three problems come up almost every day. First, a missed dose: “Have you taken your morning tablets?” Many patients forget. Second, refusing to take a pill — usually because the patient doesn't understand it. Two minutes of clear explanation fixes most of these. Third, a new side effect: “I feel sick after the new tablet.” I take that seriously, every single time. I tell them, “Call me if you notice anything unusual,” before I leave the room.

Above all, never sign for a medication you didn't actually give. Never give a medication someone else prepared without checking it yourself. The medication round is repetitive work, but inside the routine, every patient deserves the same careful attention you'd give the first one.

🤕Pain, Falls & Daily Troubles

The quiet daily problems that can ruin a recovery — pain, dizziness, falls, low mood — and how to spot them early. Useful phrases are in gold.

The Quiet Things That Can Ruin a Recovery

★ Nurse Helena Schmidt · Senior rehab nurse · Specialist in elderly care

When patients arrive in our rehab clinic, they expect to focus on big things: surgery, exercises, getting home. But what actually slows down their recovery is almost always smaller and quieter. Untreated pain. A bad night's sleep. A trip to the bathroom that ends on the floor. After fifteen years on the ward, I can tell you the daily troubles are where the real work of a rehab nurse happens.

The most important question I ask, several times a day, is about pain. “On a scale of 1 to 10, how bad is the pain right now?” Many older patients underplay their pain. They were taught not to complain. So I also watch for signs — quiet faces, slow movement, broken sleep. If a patient says “it's fine” but their score is six, I take the six seriously. I always ask, “Where exactly does it hurt?” — the location often changes the treatment.

A small problem you noticed at 8 a.m. is much easier to fix than the same problem at midnight.

Falls are the biggest fear in rehab. Most happen during a quick trip to the bathroom. Patients don't want to bother us, so they try to get up alone. That's why we say, kindly but firmly, “Please don't try to get up on your own. Ring the bell.” We check the room: clear floor, slippers in reach, walking frame nearby. A patient with the wrong shoes is a fall waiting to happen.

Dizziness is another quiet danger. After surgery or with low blood pressure, patients feel fine in bed and almost faint when they stand. I always ask, “Are you feeling dizzy?” before any movement. We sit them up slowly. We wait. Patience here prevents real injury.

Then there are the unglamorous problems. Constipation is common after surgery, painkillers and limited movement. I check politely: “When did you last open your bowels?” Many patients are embarrassed. A kind, normal tone helps. Sleep is another one. Painkillers, noise, worry, and an unfamiliar bed all damage sleep, and a tired patient cannot do good physiotherapy.

And quietly, there's the question of mood. Rehab is hard. Recovery is slow. Many patients feel low, especially in the second week when the first energy wears off. I sit down for two extra minutes. I don't try to fix anything. I just listen. When something feels seriously wrong, I don't hesitate: “I'll call the doctor straight away.” Asking for help fast is not weakness; it's good nursing.

🌙Handover & Night Shifts

How to pass on a patient safely at the end of your shift, and how to handle the special challenges of a night shift. Useful phrases are in gold.

Passing the Patient On

★ Nurse Margaret Okonkwo · Night-shift coordinator · Clinical mentor for 25 years

At the end of every shift, you give your patients to someone else. The minutes you spend on a handover are some of the most important of the whole day. A clear handover protects the patient. A rushed handover puts them at risk. In a private rehab clinic, where patients sometimes have very personal needs, the right information has to travel cleanly between three shifts: morning, evening and night.

We use a simple structure called SBAR: Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation. “Mrs Lee, room 12, post hip operation, day 4. Background: usually on two painkillers, one allergy to penicillin. Assessment: pain score 4 today, mobilising well, but a little constipated. Recommendation: continue current plan, encourage water, watch for nausea.” Twenty seconds. Everything the next nurse needs.

A handover is not a story. It is the safety belt between two nurses. Make it short, accurate and complete.

Before I leave the patient, I always say, “I'm handing over to my colleague. She'll be looking after you tonight.” Patients in private clinics often build a quick bond with their day nurse. Telling them clearly who is taking over helps them sleep better.

The night shift has its own rhythm. Most patients sleep, but not all. Some are restless. Some are in pain. Some can't sleep because they're worried about home. Before lights out, I always ask, “Is there anything you need before lights out?” Water, an extra blanket, the curtains closed differently, a question they didn't ask earlier — small things make a huge difference at 10 p.m.

I also reassure them: “I'll check on you during the night. Ring the bell if you need me.” This is not just polite. Patients who feel they're being watched safely actually sleep better and ring the bell less. Anxiety at night can quickly become real symptoms.

Night shifts also bring quieter emergencies. Confusion in older patients often gets worse after dark — we call it ‘sundowning’. A high temperature, a sudden change in breathing, low blood pressure, or a fall in the bathroom — these are the moments when you call the doctor without hesitating. “I'll call the doctor straight away.” No pride, no waiting until morning.

Finally, the family. Calls come at strange hours in private clinics. Some patients have no one. Others have ten relatives ringing in turns. When I pass on a message — “Your family called earlier; they send their love” — I always check the patient's face. A small message at night can be the most important thing that happens to them all day.
