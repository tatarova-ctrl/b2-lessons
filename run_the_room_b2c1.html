👑Leadership & Management

What the best managers do differently. Target language is in gold; idioms come later.

The Manager Who Said ‘I Don’t Know’

★ David Okonkwo · Leadership coach · Management column

For years, I believed a good manager was someone with all the answers — the boss who could solve any problem, predict any outcome, and never look uncertain. Then a quiet team leader named Priya completely changed my mind.

In a tense meeting, a junior engineer asked her a brutally hard question. Everyone expected her to bluff her way through it — that’s what managers do, right? Instead she paused and said four words I’ll never forget: “I don’t know. Let’s find out.” The room relaxed. People started talking. Within twenty minutes the team had cracked a problem that had been stuck for weeks.

That moment captures something the best leaders grasp instinctively: psychological safety — the feeling that you won’t be punished for speaking up — is the single biggest driver of team performance. When people aren’t afraid to ask questions or admit mistakes, they take smart risks. When they are afraid, they go quiet, and good ideas die in silence.

“The best managers don’t have the loudest voice. They build the room where every other voice feels safe to speak.”

Here’s the uncomfortable truth for new managers: your instinct is to micromanage. You hover, you check, you take the work back the moment it isn’t done your way. It feels responsible, but it quietly tells your team I don’t trust you. Great managers do the opposite — they delegate real responsibility and then step back, even when it’s hard to watch.

The second habit is radical candour: caring about people personally while challenging them directly. Weak managers soften feedback until it means nothing — “great job, maybe tweak a few things.” Strong ones are kind and clear at once: “This isn’t ready yet, and I know you can get it there. Here’s what’s missing.” People can handle the truth. What they can’t handle is not knowing where they stand.

Finally, the rarest skill of all: when things go wrong, great managers take ownership instead of pointing fingers. And when things go right, they give credit to the team. They lead by example rather than by title.

So if you’re stepping into management, forget the myth of the all-knowing boss. Your job isn’t to be the smartest person in the room. It’s to build a room where smart people do their best work — and sometimes that starts with three honest words: I don’t know.

💸Pricing, Inflation & Value

Why price is psychology wearing a number. Target language is in gold.

Why a £4 Coffee Feels Cheap (and a £4 App Feels Outrageous)

★ Elena Rossi · Pricing strategist · Value column

Here’s a puzzle I give every class I teach. Most people will happily pay £4 for a coffee they’ll forget in twenty minutes — yet the same people will rage about paying £4 for an app they’ll use every day for years. Why? The answer reveals almost everything about how pricing really works, and here’s the twist: it has very little to do with cost.

Customers don’t judge price against what something costs to make. They judge it against what it feels worth — what economists call perceived value. A coffee comes with a warm cup, a smell, a ritual, a smiling barista. An app is invisible. Same price, completely different feeling.

This is why smart companies obsess over anchoring. Show someone a £900 laptop beside a £1,400 one, and suddenly the £900 feels reasonable — even if you only planned to spend £600. The expensive option isn’t really there to sell. It’s there to make everything else look like a bargain.

“Price isn’t a number on a label. It’s a story your customer tells themselves about what they’re getting.”

Then there’s the trick everyone has noticed but few can name: shrinkflation. Instead of raising the price, companies quietly shrink the product. The chocolate bar gets thinner; the crisp packet holds more air. The price point stays the same, so customers feel safe — even though they’re paying more per gram. Clever, and a little sneaky.

Now the strategy that surprises my students most: going up, not down. We assume cheaper always wins. But premium positioning — deliberately charging more — can actually increase demand, because a high price signals quality. A £2 watch feels like a toy; the same watch at £200 feels like a statement. Slashing your price to undercut rivals can quietly tell the market your product is cheap in every sense.

Of course, none of this escapes inflation. When prices rise across the board, customers feel sticker shock — that jolt of surprise at the till — and businesses face a brutal choice: raise prices and risk losing people, or absorb the cost and watch profits shrink.

The lesson? Price is psychology wearing a number. Before you argue that something is “too expensive,” ask the better question: expensive compared to what — and worth it to whom? Get that right, and you’ll never look at a price tag the same way again.

📈Project Management

Why projects really run late — and it isn’t the software. Target language is in gold.

The Project That Was ‘90% Done’ for Six Months

★ Tom Hayes · Delivery lead · Project column

Every experienced project manager knows the joke, because they’ve lived it: the project that’s been “90% finished” for half a year. The final 10% never seems to arrive. Understanding why is one of the most useful things you can learn in business — and it’s far more about people than spreadsheets.

The first culprit is scope creep. A project starts simple. Then someone says, “While we’re at it, could we just add…” — and suddenly the small website has a login, a chatbot and a loyalty scheme. Each request sounds harmless. Together, they quietly sink the timeline. Good managers learn to say a polite but firm no, or to deprioritise extras for later.

The second is the planning fallacy: our stubborn human habit of assuming everything will go perfectly. We estimate the best case and forget that people get sick, suppliers fall behind, and the one person who understands the database goes on holiday. That’s why smart teams build in buffers and break work into short sprints instead of one giant push.

“A deadline isn’t the day the work ends. It’s the day you stop pretending the surprises won’t happen.”

Then there are bottlenecks — the single slow step that holds everything up. You can have ten brilliant people, but if every deliverable must pass through one overloaded approver, the whole team moves at that person’s speed. Finding and fixing bottlenecks is often the fastest way to rescue a struggling project.

This is where agile thinking changed the game. Instead of planning everything up front and hoping, agile teams work in small cycles, show real progress often, and adjust as they learn. They’d rather be roughly right and flexible than precisely wrong and stuck.

But my favourite tool is the simplest: the retrospective. After each phase the team asks three honest questions — what went well, what went badly, and what we’ll do differently. No blame, just learning. The teams that get to the bottom of their mistakes don’t repeat them.

Here’s the deeper truth, though. Projects rarely fail because of bad software or weak plans. They fail because people overpromise to avoid awkward conversations, hide problems until it’s too late, and confuse looking busy with making progress. The best project managers aren’t the best planners — they’re the bravest communicators, willing to say early and clearly, “We’re not actually at 90%. Let’s talk about the real number.”

The Best Pitch I Ever Saw Had No Slides

★ Sofia Marín · Communication coach · Presentation column

I’ve watched more than a thousand business pitches, and the best one I ever saw broke every rule I’d been taught. No slick pitch deck. No charts. No laser pointer. The founder simply stood up, looked at the investors, and said: “Last year, my grandmother nearly took the wrong medication. This app makes sure that never happens to your family.” Then she paused. You could have heard a pin drop. She’d won the room over in fifteen seconds — before mentioning a single feature.

That’s the secret nobody tells you: people don’t buy information, they buy a story. Facts inform, but stories move. The strongest pitches open with a hook — a moment, a question, a surprising number — that makes the audience need to hear what comes next.

The second skill is learning to read the room. Average presenters deliver the same speech regardless of who’s listening. Great ones watch faces, notice when eyes glaze over, and tailor their message on the spot. If the room is bored, they cut to the chase. If it’s curious, they slow down and go deeper.

“Nobody remembers your data. They remember how you made them feel about your data.”

Here’s a trap even experts fall into — the curse of knowledge. When you know your subject inside out, you forget what it’s like not to. You drown people in jargon, and your brilliant idea falls flat. The cure is brutal simplicity: if a twelve-year-old couldn’t repeat your main point, it isn’t clear enough yet.

Then comes the part most people fear: questions. But tough questions are a gift — they mean people are engaged. Strong presenters don’t panic or waffle. They pause, answer honestly, and aren’t afraid to say “I don’t know, but I’ll find out.” Confidence isn’t knowing everything; it’s being calm when you don’t.

Finally, every great pitch ends with a clear call to action. So many presentations simply… stop. The lights come up and nobody knows what to do next. Don’t leave your audience guessing. Tell them exactly what you want: “Here’s the one decision I’m asking you to make today.”

The good news? None of this requires charisma you were born with. It takes preparation, empathy, and the courage to be human in a suit. Strip away the buzzwords and a great pitch is just one person, telling one true story, asking for one clear thing. Master that, and you’ll nail presentations long after everyone’s forgotten their slides.
