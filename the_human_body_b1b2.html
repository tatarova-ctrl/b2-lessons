🦴The Body & Common Injuries

Body parts and the everyday accidents that send people to the clinic. Useful action verbs are in gold.

The Day My Leg Said No

★ Nurse Marta Costa · A&E and rehab clinic · Sees 30 injuries a week

I work in a private rehabilitation clinic. Every morning starts the same way: patients walk in with a story. “I was cooking dinner and I cut my finger.” “I fell down the stairs last night.” “I twisted my ankle playing football on Sunday.” Most accidents at home or in sport are small, but they teach us a lot about the human body.

Let's start at the top. The head contains the brain and the face. Patients often bang their head on cupboard doors or low ceilings. Below the head is the neck, and from the shoulders down the arms there are the shoulder, the elbow, the wrist, the hand and the fingers. Patients often sprain a wrist in a fall, or dislocate a shoulder in a sport like rugby. To ‘sprain’ means to stretch or tear the soft tissue around a joint. To ‘dislocate’ means a bone has moved out of its normal place.

Patients rarely arrive in pain because of the body. They arrive because something the body normally does without thinking has suddenly become difficult.

The middle of the body is the chest and the back. Back pain is one of the most common reasons people visit a rehab clinic. Lifting heavy things badly, sitting at a screen for hours, or sleeping in a strange position can all cause back trouble. Then we have the hips, the knees, the ankles and the feet. The knee is one of the busiest joints in the body, and one of the easiest to damage. Older patients break a hip, runners tear a ligament, footballers twist a knee.

On the skin, simple injuries are everywhere. A cut from a kitchen knife. A bruise after bumping into a corner. A burn from a hot pan. A graze on a child's knee. Most of these heal on their own, but we still clean them, check them and make sure there is no infection. A small wound that gets infected can ruin a holiday.

Then there are the big accidents. A broken arm after falling off a bike. A fractured rib after a car accident. A head injury after a fall on ice. These patients need an X-ray quickly, and sometimes a hospital stay before they come to us for rehabilitation.

The most important thing we teach patients is calm. Don't panic. Don't try to walk on a leg that hurts. Sit down, call for help, breathe slowly. The body is strong. With the right care, even a serious injury can heal beautifully — especially in the first 48 hours, when how you act really matters.

❤️Inside the Body

The quiet workers: the major organs and the diseases that affect them. Target vocabulary is in gold.

The Quiet Workers

★ Dr Vincenzo Romano · Internal medicine specialist · 25 years in private practice

When you wake up, your body has already done thousands of important things without asking you. The heart has beaten around 25,000 times. The lungs have moved millions of litres of air. The liver, the kidneys and the brain have cleaned, filtered and processed without a break. Most patients only think about their internal organs when something stops working. That's the moment they walk into my office.

Let's start with the heart. The heart is a muscle the size of your fist that pumps blood around the body. When the arteries that feed it get blocked, the result is a heart attack. Patients often describe it as “a heavy weight on the chest.” Other big problems include high blood pressure (‘hypertension’) and an irregular heartbeat. Most heart disease is partly genetic and partly lifestyle: smoking, food, stress and lack of movement.

The organs that hurt loudly are the ones we usually save. The ones that suffer in silence are the ones that kill us.

Above the heart sit the lungs. They bring oxygen into the blood and remove carbon dioxide. Common diseases include pneumonia (a serious infection of the lungs), asthma (when the airways tighten) and the long-term damage from smoking known as COPD. A patient who is short of breath at rest should never be ignored.

Below the lungs we find the stomach, the liver and the intestines. The stomach can develop a stomach ulcer — a painful sore in its lining. The liver can become inflamed in hepatitis, or scarred in cirrhosis, often from too much alcohol. Patients with stomach problems often delay coming in, hoping the pain will pass. It usually doesn't.

Then there are the kidneys, two bean-shaped organs that filter your blood. Hard pieces can form inside them, called kidney stones, and the pain when they move is famously one of the worst pains in medicine. Diabetes is also a major problem here: too much sugar in the blood, over years, slowly damages the kidneys, the eyes, and the small blood vessels everywhere in the body.

And finally, the brain. A stroke happens when blood stops flowing to part of the brain. Speed is everything: every minute, brain cells die. We tell families the simple FAST test: Face dropping, Arm weakness, Speech problems, Time to call an ambulance. The faster they call, the better the recovery.

Most serious internal diseases are not sudden lightning strikes. They are quiet stories that develop over years. The best gift you can give your patients is not panic — it is the calm habit of yearly check-ups, simple blood tests, and the courage to come early. The body whispers long before it shouts. Our job is to teach patients to listen.

🔬Scans, Tests & Diagnoses

What modern tests really show — and how to explain them in clear English to a nervous patient. Target language is in gold.

What the Pictures See That You Can't

★ Dr Sarah Olusoga · Radiologist · Twenty years reading scans

Modern medicine is built on pictures and numbers. Two hundred years ago, a doctor could only look, listen and touch. Today we can see inside the human body with extraordinary clarity. As a radiologist, I read scans every day. The technology is impressive, but the most important moment is still the one where I explain the results to a patient in language they can understand.

The simplest and oldest scan is the X-ray. It uses radiation to make a picture, especially of bones. If you fall on the ice and hurt your wrist, the first question is always: “Is anything broken?” An X-ray gives that answer in two minutes. It's not perfect for soft tissue, but for bones it is still our best friend.

For more detailed images we use a CT scan (computed tomography). It takes many X-rays from different angles and a computer turns them into thin “slices” of the body. We use CT for chest problems, head injuries, internal bleeding and suspected cancers. It's fast, which is why every emergency department has one.

A scan does not give us a diagnosis. It gives us a clue. The diagnosis still comes from a careful conversation with the patient.

An MRI scan (magnetic resonance imaging) uses powerful magnets rather than radiation. It is brilliant for soft tissue: the brain, the spinal cord, joints, muscles, the heart. It is also noisy and slow — some patients find the closed tube difficult. We always check beforehand if they have metal in their body, because metal and strong magnets do not get along.

An ultrasound is the friendly relative of the scan family: no radiation, no noise, just sound waves and a clear gel. It is famous for pregnancy, but we also use it for the heart, the abdomen and the thyroid. It's safe, cheap and very informative.

Then come the tests with a camera at the end of a small tube. A gastroscopy looks at the stomach and the upper part of the digestive system — useful when we suspect an ulcer or a problem swallowing. A colonoscopy looks at the lower bowel and is a key test for bowel cancer. Many patients dread it, but it's the single test that has saved the most lives in the last thirty years.

Finally, the simplest investigations are still some of the best. A blood test can tell us about anaemia, infection, diabetes, kidney function and the liver. A urine test can find an infection in two minutes. An ECG records the electrical activity of the heart and takes only five minutes. Sometimes a biopsy — taking a tiny piece of tissue — is needed for a final answer.

When patients come for results, I try to remember one thing. The numbers and pictures are mine; the body is theirs. My job is to explain, in normal words, what the picture means and what will happen next. Behind every scan is a person hoping for good news — or hoping to be told the bad news kindly.

🩺Who's Who at the Clinic

How to send a patient to the right specialist — and explain to them, in plain English, who does what. Useful words are in gold.

The Right Door for the Right Problem

★ Dr Henrik Bergström · GP and clinic director · Coordinates 14 specialists

If you visit a private clinic with a problem, the first person you usually meet is a GP — a general practitioner, or family doctor. The GP is the front door of medicine. Their job is to listen, examine, look at your history and decide what to do next. Sometimes the answer is a tablet and a follow-up. Sometimes the answer is a referral — a letter to a specialist who has spent years learning one specific part of the body.

Specialists exist because the human body is too big and too complicated for one doctor to know everything. After medical school, a young doctor chooses a path and spends another five, six, sometimes ten years training in one area. By the time they treat patients, they have seen thousands of cases of one type of problem.

A good GP is not the doctor who knows everything. A good GP is the doctor who knows exactly who to send you to.

Let me walk you through the most common doors at our clinic. A paediatrician looks after children, from new-born babies to teenagers. A gynaecologist takes care of women's health, especially the reproductive system, while an obstetrician specialises in pregnancy and birth. A dermatologist treats the skin, hair and nails. They are the people you see for a worrying mole, persistent acne, or a strange rash.

If your problem is the heart, you'll meet a cardiologist. If your problem is the digestive system — stomach, intestines, liver — you'll be sent to a gastroenterologist. A neurologist deals with the brain and the nervous system, including headaches, epilepsy and stroke care. An orthopaedic surgeon treats bones, joints and sports injuries, which means our rehab clinic talks to them every single day. An ENT specialist (ear, nose and throat) deals with hearing, sinus problems and the throat.

Other essential doctors are easy to forget. An internist — the European version of an internal medicine specialist — manages adult patients with multiple chronic conditions. A urologist treats kidneys, bladder and male reproductive health. An endocrinologist looks after hormones, including thyroid problems and diabetes. A psychiatrist treats mental health from a medical angle, while a psychologist focuses on therapy and behaviour. And of course, the dentist looks after the teeth and gums — the door we should all walk through twice a year, but most of us avoid.

When a nurse phones a specialist on behalf of a patient, the conversation is short, clear and structured. “I have a 58-year-old man with chest pain and a family history of heart disease. We've done an ECG and a blood test. Can he see Dr Kovacs today?” Two minutes. Clean information. A decision. That's how a private clinic works at its best: the patient walks through one door, and inside, we quietly find them the right one.
